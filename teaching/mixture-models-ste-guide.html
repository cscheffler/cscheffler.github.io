2. Monsters and mixtures

Main point: We can make new models by joining parts of simpler models. These models are powerful, but they are more difficult to fit and to understand. Always test a new model on simulated data.

Many monsters in stories and legends are hybrids: they have parts of different animals. Many statistical models are also hybrids. This chapter makes likelihood functions and link functionsᴳ from the simpler parts of the earlier chapters. These hybrid likelihoods have some properties of each part. Thus, they can model outcome variables with properties that are inconvenient, but common.

These models are both powerful and dangerous. They are often more difficult to estimate and to understand. But with some knowledge and care, they are important tools.

This paper shows two of the three types of model in the chapter:

  1. Over-dispersed counts. These models extend the binomial and Poisson models of the previous chapter. They include some of the variation from sources that we did not measure (Sections 3 to 6).

  2. Zero-inflated and zero-augmented models. Each of these models mixes a binary event with an ordinary GLM likelihood, for example a Poisson or a binomial likelihood (Sections 7 to 9).

The third type, the ordered categorical model, is in Section 12.3 in the textbook. We will not cover it here.

These model types let us change our models to agree with the inconvenient facts of measurement. We do not change the measurements to agree with the limits of our models.

There are many other model types of this kind. This paper cannot show all of them. But when you see a new type, you have a method to understand it. You can also make your own new model. But always do these steps to test it:

  1. Simulate dummy data from a known data-generating process.

  2. Fit the model to the dummy data.

  3. Make sure that the model recovers the known values of the process.

3. Over-dispersed counts

Main point: When counts come from a mixture of different processes, they have more variation than a pure count model expects. This is over-dispersion. A continuous mixture model can include this extra variation.

3.1 Thick tails in count models

Chapter 7 in the textbook shows that models with normal distributions can be too sensitive to extreme observations. The problem is not that "outliers" are bad data. Many processes are variable mixtures, and mixtures give thicker tails. A model that assumes thin tails, for example a pure Gaussian model, can become "excited" too easily. A Student's t distribution can give better inferences and better out-of-sample predictions.

The same problem occurs with count models. When counts come from a mixture of different processes, the counts can have more variation (thicker tails) than a pure count model expects. Then the model can also become too excited.

3.2 Dispersion and over-dispersion

The variance of a variable is sometimes called its dispersionᴳ. For a counting process, for example a binomial process, the variance is a function of the same parameters as the expected value:

expected value=Npvariance=Np(1−p)\text{expected value} = Np \qquad \text{variance} = Np(1-p)

Sometimes the observed variance is larger than this amount, after we condition on all the predictor variables. This is over-dispersionᴳ. Over-dispersion tells us that a variable that is not in the model causes additional dispersion in the observed counts.

Over-dispersion is not always bad. A model with over-dispersion can still give good inferences. But if you ignore over-dispersion, you can get the same problems as when you ignore any predictor variable. Variation in the counts (heterogeneityᴳ) can be a confound. It can hide effects that are important, or it can cause false (spurious) inferences. Thus, it is important to work with over-dispersion.

3.3 Two strategies

The best solution is to find the missing source of dispersion and to put it in the model. But you can decrease the effects of over-dispersion even when you do not have more variables. There are two common strategies:

  1. Use a continuous mixture model. In this type of model, the linear model does not attach to the observations. It attaches to a distribution of observations. Sections 4 to 6 show two models of this type: the beta-binomial modelᴳ and the gamma-Poisson modelᴳ. The gamma-Poisson model is also called the negative-binomial modelᴳ.

  2. Use a multilevel modelᴳ. A multilevel model estimates the residual of each observation and also the distribution of these residuals. In practice, multilevel models are often easier to use than continuous mixtures, because they are much more flexible. They can include over-dispersion and other types of heterogeneity at the same time. We will learn about these models in the next class session.

4. Beta-binomial

Main point: A beta-binomial model gives each binomial count its own probability of success. The model estimates the distribution of these probabilities. The predictor variables change the shape of this distribution.

4.1 The idea

A beta-binomial model assumes that each binomial count observation has its own probability of success. The model does not estimate one probability of success. It estimates the distribution of the probabilities of success across the cases. The predictor variables change the shape of this distribution. They do not directly set the probability of each success.

An example makes this easier to understand. The UCBadmit data are from the paper about binomial regression (Section 11.1 in the textbook). These data are very over-dispersed if we ignore the department. The cause is that the departments have very different baseline admission rates. When we ignored this variation, we got an incorrect inference about the gender of the applicants. Now we fit a beta-binomial model. We ignore the department again, and we see how the model finds the variation that comes from the missing variable.

The beta-binomial model of these data assumes that:

  • Each observed count, on each row of the data table, has its own probability of admission. We cannot observe this probability.

  • These probabilities of admission have a common distribution. This distribution is a beta distributionᴳ.

A beta distribution is a probability distribution for probabilities. We use a beta distribution because it makes the mathematics easy. With a beta distribution, we can calculate a closed-formᴳ likelihood function. This likelihood averages over the unknown probabilities of the observations. Section 6.2 gives the details.

4.2 The parameters of the beta distribution

In this paper, a beta distribution has two parameters:

  • an average probability p̄ ("p bar")

  • a shape parameterᴳ θ ("theta")

The shape parameter θ tells how spread out the distribution is:

  • When θ = 2, each probability from 0 to 1 is equally likely. The distribution is flat.

  • When θ is more than 2, the distribution of probabilities becomes more concentrated.

  • When θ is less than 2, the distribution is so dispersed that extreme probabilities near 0 and 1 are more likely than the mean.

SciPy uses two different parameters, a and b. Change p̄ and θ into SciPy's parameters with a = p̄θ and b = (1 − p̄)θ. The function below does this. Use it to see the shapes that the distribution can have:

def beta2_pdf(x, pbar, theta):
    """Beta density with mean pbar and shape theta."""
    return stats.beta.pdf(x, pbar * theta, (1 - pbar) * theta)

x = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 300)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5, 3.4))
for theta, ls in [(1, ":"), (2, "-."), (5, "-"), (20, "--")]:
    ax.plot(x, beta2_pdf(x, 0.5, theta), color=DARK, ls=ls, lw=1.8, label=f"theta = {theta}")
ax.set_ylim(0, 4)
ax.set_xlabel("probability")
ax.set_ylabel("density")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
plt.show()

Figure 1. Beta distributions with p̄ = 0.5. When θ = 1, the probabilities near 0 and 1 are the most likely. When θ = 2, the distribution is flat. When θ = 5 and θ = 20, the distribution is more and more concentrated near 0.5.

Try different values for pbar and theta. Remember that this is a distribution for probabilities. The horizontal axis shows the possible probability values. The vertical axis shows the density for each probability, when we sample probabilities from the distribution. This is strange at first, but you will become familiar with it.

4.3 The model

We attach the linear model to p̄. Thus, when the predictor variables change, the central tendency of the distribution changes. The model is:

Ai∼BetaBinomial(Ni,pˉi,θ)logit(pˉi)=αGID[i]αj∼Normal(0,1.5)θ=ϕ+2ϕ∼Exponential(1)\begin{aligned} A_i &\sim \text{BetaBinomial}(N_i, \bar{p}_i, \theta) \\ \text{logit}(\bar{p}_i) &= \alpha_{\text{GID}[i]} \\ \alpha_j &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 1.5) \\ \theta &= \phi + 2 \\ \phi &\sim \text{Exponential}(1) \end{aligned}

The outcome A is admit. The size N is applications. GID[i] is the gender index. In the Python code, the index is 0 for male and 1 for female.

The prior for θ uses a trick. We want to assume that θ is 2 or more. This means that the distribution is flat or more concentrated. If θ is less than 2, the distribution puts probability near 0 and 1. If θ is more than 2, the distribution puts more and more probability near one value. We need a distribution with a minimum of 2. The exponential distribution has a minimum of 0. If you add 2 to a variable with an exponential distribution, the new variable has a minimum of 2. Thus, the model gives φ an exponential distribution and then sets θ = φ + 2.

4.4 Fit the model

This code loads the data and fits the beta-binomial model. PyMC's BetaBinomial uses the parameters alpha and beta, so we change p̄ and θ into these parameters again. The pm.Deterministic line keeps theta in the posterior samples.

ucb = pd.read_csv(DATA + "UCBadmit.csv", sep=";")
gid = (ucb["applicant.gender"] == "female").astype(int).values   # 0 = male, 1 = female
A = ucb["admit"].values
N = ucb["applications"].values

with pm.Model(coords={"gender": ["male", "female"]}) as m12_1:
    a = pm.Normal("a", 0, 1.5, dims="gender")
    phi = pm.Exponential("phi", 1)
    theta = pm.Deterministic("theta", phi + 2)        # theta is 2 or more
    pbar = pm.math.invlogit(a[gid])
    pm.BetaBinomial("A", n=N, alpha=pbar * theta, beta=(1 - pbar) * theta, observed=A)
    idata_12_1 = pm.sample(1000, chains=4, random_seed=1, target_accept=0.95)

The option target_accept=0.95 makes the sampler take smaller steps. This prevents divergent transitions. We will learn more about divergent transitions next week.

4.5 Interpret the result

The parameter a[male] is the log-odds of admission for male applicants. It is lower than a[female], the log-odds for female applicants. But the difference da is very uncertain. There is not much evidence of a difference between the male and female admission rates.

In the paper about binomial regression, a binomial model that did not include the department gave a misleading result. There is an indirect path from gender through department to admission. This confound gave a false indication that female applicants had lower odds of admission. The beta-binomial model above does not include the department, but it is not confounded. Why?

The beta-binomial model lets each row in the data (each combination of department and gender) have its own intercept. We cannot observe these intercepts. 5. Gamma-Poisson

Main point: A gamma-Poisson model gives each Poisson count its own rate. The rates have a gamma distribution. This model expects more variation than a pure Poisson model. Thus, extreme points have less influence on it.

5.1 The idea

A negative-binomial model assumes that each Poisson count observation has its own rate. A better name for this model is the gamma-Poisson model. The model estimates the shape of a gamma distributionᴳ, and this distribution gives the Poisson rates across the cases. The predictor variables change the shape of this distribution. They do not change the expected value of each observation directly.

The gamma-Poisson model is very similar to the beta-binomial model. A gamma distribution of rates (expected values) replaces the beta distribution of probabilities of success. We use a gamma distribution because it makes the mathematics easy. There is a simple analytical expression for Poisson probabilities that are mixed with rates from a gamma distribution.

Gamma-Poisson models are very useful because Poisson distributions are very narrow. Remember that the variance of a Poisson distribution must be equal to its mean.

5.2 The parameters

The gamma-Poisson distribution has two parameters. One parameter is the mean (the rate). The other parameter is the dispersion (the scale) of the rates across the cases.

yi∼Gamma-Poisson(λi,ϕ)y_i \sim \text{Gamma-Poisson}(\lambda_i, \phi)

Use the parameter λ like the rate of an ordinary Poisson distribution. The parameter φ must be positive. It controls the variance:

variance=λ+λ2ϕ\text{variance} = \lambda + \frac{\lambda^2}{\phi}

Thus, when φ is larger, the distribution is more similar to a pure Poisson process.

In PyMC, the gamma-Poisson distribution is pm.NegativeBinomial. Its parameter mu is λ, and its parameter alpha is φ.

5.3 Example: the Oceanic tools

We use the Oceanic tools data from the section about Poisson regression (Section 11.2 in the textbook). In the pure Poisson model m11_11, Hawaii was a very influential point. In the gamma-Poisson model, Hawaii becomes much less influential. Why? The gamma-Poisson model expects more variation around the mean rate. Thus, Hawaii pulls the regression trend less.

The code below fits the pure Poisson model m11_11 again, for comparison. Then it fits the gamma-Poisson model m12_2. The two models use the same scientific model for λ.

d = pd.read_csv(DATA + "Kline2.csv")
T = d["total_tools"].values
Pop = d["population"].values
cid = (d["contact"] == "high").astype(int).values    # 0 = low contact, 1 = high contact
coords = {"contact": ["low", "high"]}

# the pure Poisson model from the previous paper
with pm.Model(coords=coords) as m11_11:
    a = pm.Normal("a", 1, 1, dims="contact")
    b = pm.Exponential("b", 1, dims="contact")
    g = pm.Exponential("g", 1)
    lam = pm.math.exp(a[cid]) * Pop ** b[cid] / g
    pm.Poisson("T", mu=lam, observed=T)
    idata_11_11 = pm.sample(1000, chains=4, random_seed=1, target_accept=0.99,
                            idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True})

# the gamma-Poisson model

                           idata_kwargs={"log_likelihood": True})

6. Over-dispersion, entropy and information criteria

Main point: For model comparison, a beta-binomial model is a binomial model, and a gamma-Poisson model is a Poisson model. But do not use WAIC or PSIS with these models, unless you are very sure of what you do.

6.1 Do not use WAIC or PSIS with these models

The beta-binomial and gamma-Poisson models are maximum entropyᴳ distributions for the same constraints as the ordinary binomial and Poisson distributions. They only try to include unobserved heterogeneity in the probabilities and rates. They can be much more difficult to fit to data. But you can think about them in the same way as ordinary binomial and Poisson GLMs. Thus, for model comparison with information criteria, a beta-binomial model is a binomial model, and a gamma-Poisson (negative-binomial) model is a Poisson model.

But do not use WAIC and PSIS with these models, unless you are very sure of what you do. The reason is this:

  • You can aggregateᴳ or disaggregate ordinary binomial and Poisson models across the rows in the data. This does not change the causal assumptions.

  • This is not true for beta-binomial and gamma-Poisson models. A beta-binomial or gamma-Poisson likelihood applies an unobserved parameter to each row in the data. When you calculate the log-likelihoods, the structure of the data controls how the beta or gamma variation goes into the model.

For example, the beta-binomial model in Section 4 has counts on each row. Each row is a combination of department and gender. The model assumes that all the applications for one combination have the same unknown baseline probability of acceptance. We want to use each application as an observation, and calculate WAIC over the applications. Then we get an estimate of the accuracy for a new application to a known department and gender. We can disaggregate the data, so that each row is one application. But then we lose an important part of the model. The beta-binomial model says that all the applicants from the same row have the same latent probability. Thus, this is a large problem.

What can you do? Multilevel models, the topic of the next class session, make this problem smaller. A multilevel model can include over-dispersion, and it can put heterogeneity in probabilities or rates at any level of aggregation.

6.2 Continuous mixtures

A distribution like the beta-binomial is a continuous mixtureᴳ. Each binomial count has its own independent probability of success from a beta distribution. The beta distribution is continuous, not discrete. Thus, the parameters of the beta-binomial distribution are:

  • the number of draws in each case (the same as the size n of the ordinary binomial distribution)

  • the two parameters that give the shape of the beta distribution, p̄ and θ

Thus, the probability of y successes from a beta-binomial process is:

f(y∣n,pˉ,θ)=∫01g(y∣n,p) h(p∣pˉ,θ) dpf(y \mid n, \bar{p}, \theta) = \int_0^1 g(y \mid n, p) \, h(p \mid \bar{p}, \theta) \, dp

In this equation:

  • f is the beta-binomial density.

  • g is the binomial distribution.

  • h is the beta density.

This integral, like most integrals in applied probability, calculates an average. It gives the probability of y, averaged over all the values of p. The values of p come from the beta distribution with mean p̄ and scale θ. The probability of success p is not a free parameter, because the beta distribution makes it.

The gamma-Poisson density has a similar form. It averages a Poisson probability over a gamma distribution of rates.

For the beta-binomial and the gamma-Poisson distributions, you can solve the integral above. Thus, they have closed-form expressions. You can find these expressions when you need them. In PyMC, pm.BetaBinomial and pm.NegativeBinomial calculate them for you. In SciPy, use stats.betabinom and stats.nbinom.

7. Zero-inflated outcomes

Main point: When different causes can give the same observation, use a mixture model. Counts of zero often have more than one cause.

7.1 Mixture models

Frequently, the things that we measure do not come from one pure process. They are mixtures of many processes. When different causes can give the same observation, a mixture modelᴳ can be useful. A mixture model uses more than one simple probability distribution to model a mixture of causes. Thus, these models use more than one likelihood for the same outcome variable.

Count variables frequently need a mixture model. The reason is that a count of zero can often occur in more than one way. A zero means that nothing occurred. Nothing can occur for two reasons:

  • The rate of events is low.

  • The process that makes the events did not start.

For example, we count scrub jays (a type of bird) in a forest. We can record a zero because there are no scrub jays in the forest. We can also record a zero because we frightened all the birds away before we started to look. In both cases, the data contain a zero.

Section 8 shows how to make a simple zero-inflatedᴳ model. It uses the same parts as the earlier models, but it joins them in a different way. You possibly will never need to use a zero-inflated model. But when you see how to make one, you will have more ideas for your own models.

7.2 Breaking the law

In the sciences, some researchers are anxious about statistical inference. In the past, researchers could not easily make and study their own custom models. They had to wait for statisticians to study the models first. Thus, researchers were concerned about unconventional models. They were concerned that they "broke the laws of statistics".

Now statistical computing is much more powerful. You can do these steps yourself:

  1. Think of your own generative processᴳ.

  2. Simulate data from it.

  3. Write the model.

  4. Make sure that the model recovers the true parameter values.

You do not have to wait for a mathematician to make your model "legal".

8. Example: zero-inflated Poisson

Main point: A zero-inflated Poisson model mixes a binary process (drink or work) with a Poisson process (the number of manuscripts). The model can estimate how often the binary event occurs, but it cannot tell which zeros come from which process.

8.1 The drinking monks

The paper about Poisson regression (Section 11.2 in the textbook) used the example of a monastery that makes manuscripts. Each day, a large number of monks copy manuscripts. They complete a small number of manuscripts. The process is a binomial process, but with a large number of trials and a very low probability. Thus, the distribution is almost a Poisson distribution.

Now think that the monks take breaks on some days. On these days, they complete no manuscripts. They open the wine cellar and they drink. You own the monastery, and you want to know how often the monks drink. The problem for inference is this: the monks also make zeros on days when they work, by chance. How can you estimate the number of days that the monks drink?

Make a mixture model to solve this problem. Each zero in the data can come from two processes:

  1. The monks drank on that day.

  2. The monks worked on that day, but they did not complete a manuscript.

Let p be the probability that the monks drink on a day. Let λ be the mean number of manuscripts that the monks complete when they work.

8.2 The likelihood

We need a likelihood function that mixes the two processes. Think of the drinking as the result of a coin toss (Figure 4). One side of the "coin" shows a wine cask. The other side shows a quill pen. The probability of the wine cask is p, and p can have any value from 0 to 1. The result of the coin toss tells the monks to drink or to copy. Drinking monks always complete zero manuscripts. Working monks complete a Poisson number of manuscripts, with an average rate λ. Thus, you can observe a zero also when the monks work.

Figure 4. The structure of the zero-inflated likelihood. Start at the top. The monks drink on a proportion p of the days, or they work on a proportion 1 − p of the days. Drinking monks always give the observation y = 0. Working monks can give y = 0 or y > 0.

With these assumptions, the likelihood of a zero is:

Pr⁡(0∣p,λ)=Pr⁡(drink∣p)+Pr⁡(work∣p)×Pr⁡(0∣λ)=p+(1−p)exp⁡(−λ)\begin{aligned} \Pr(0 \mid p, \lambda) &= \Pr(\text{drink} \mid p) + \Pr(\text{work} \mid p) \times \Pr(0 \mid \lambda) \\ &= p + (1 - p) \exp(-\lambda) \end{aligned}

The Poisson likelihood of y is Pr(y | λ) = λ^y exp(−λ) / y!. Thus, the likelihood of y = 0 is exp(−λ). The equation above says this:

The probability of a zero is the probability that the monks drank OR (+) the probability that the monks worked AND (×) did not complete a manuscript.

The likelihood of a value y that is not zero is:

Pr⁡(y∣y>0,p,λ)=Pr⁡(drink∣p)(0)+Pr⁡(work∣p)Pr⁡(y∣λ)=(1−p)λyexp⁡(−λ)y!\Pr(y \mid y > 0, p, \lambda) = \Pr(\text{drink} \mid p)(0) + \Pr(\text{work} \mid p)\Pr(y \mid \lambda) = (1 - p)\frac{\lambda^y \exp(-\lambda)}{y!}

Drinking monks never give y > 0. Thus, this equation is the probability that the monks work, 1 − p, and complete y manuscripts.

8.3 The model

Let ZIPoisson be the distribution above. Its parameters are p (the probability of a zero from the binary process) and λ (the mean of the Poisson process). A zero-inflated Poisson regression has this form:

yi∼ZIPoisson(pi,λi)logit(pi)=αp+βpxilog⁡(λi)=αλ+βλxi\begin{aligned} y_i &\sim \text{ZIPoisson}(p_i, \lambda_i) \\ \text{logit}(p_i) &= \alpha_p + \beta_p x_i \\ \log(\lambda_i) &= \alpha_\lambda + \beta_\lambda x_i \end{aligned}

There are two linear models and two link functions, one for each process in the ZIPoisson. The two linear models have different parameters, because a predictor such as x can have a different association with each part of the mixture. You do not have to use the same predictors in the two models. Make the two linear models as your hypothesis needs.

8.4 Simulate the data

We need data. Simulate the drinking and the work of the monks. Then fit a model to recover the parameter values of the simulation.

prob_drink = 0.2      # 20% of days
rate_work = 1         # average 1 manuscript per day

N_days = 365          # one year of production
drink = rng.binomial(1, prob_drink, N_days)          # days that the monks drink
y = (1 - drink) * rng.poisson(rate_work, N_days)    # manuscripts completed

We can observe only the outcome variable y. It is a list of counts of completed manuscripts, one count for each day of the year. Look at the outcome variable:

zeros_drink = drink.sum()
zeros_work = np.sum((y == 0) & (drink == 0))
zeros_total = np.sum(y == 0)
counts = np.bincount(y)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.5, 3.4))
ax.vlines(np.arange(len(counts)), 0, counts, color=DARK, lw=5)
ax.vlines(0, zeros_work, zeros_total, color=BLUE, lw=5)    # zeros from drinking
ax.set_xlabel("manuscripts completed")
ax.set_ylabel("frequency")
plt.show()
print(zeros_drink, zeros_work, zeros_total)

Figure 5. The frequency distribution of the zero-inflated observations. The blue segment above zero shows the y = 0 observations from drinking. The black part of the bar shows the zeros from work. In real data, we usually cannot see which zeros come from which process.

In this simulation, 66 zeros come from drinking and 108 zeros come from work. There are 174 zeros in total. The total number of zeros is inflated, if you compare it to a typical Poisson distribution.

8.5 Fit the model

PyMC has the zero-inflated Poisson likelihood pm.ZeroInflatedPoisson. Be careful with its parameter psi. In PyMC, psi is the probability of the Poisson process (work). Thus, psi = 1 − p. Section 9 shows how this likelihood relates to the mathematics above.

The prior for ap moves the probability of drinking so that there is more probability below 0.5 than above 0.5. The monks probably do not drink on more than half of the days.

with pm.Model() as m12_3:
    ap = pm.Normal("ap", -1.5, 1)
    al = pm.Normal("al", 1, 0.5)
    p = pm.math.invlogit(ap)                  # probability of drinking
    lam = pm.math.exp(al)                     # rate of work
    # PyMC uses psi = the probability of the Poisson process, so psi = 1 - p
    pm.ZeroInflatedPoisson("y", psi=1 - p, mu=lam, observed=y)
    idata_12_3 = pm.sample(1000, chains=4, random_seed=1)

az.summary(idata_12_3, var_names=["ap", "al"], ci_prob=0.89).round(2)

We get an accurate estimate of the proportion of days that the monks drink. But we cannot say if the monks drank on a specified day.

This example is the simplest possible example. In real problems, you can have predictor variables that have an association with one or both processes in the zero-inflated Poisson mixture. Then add these variables and their parameters to one or both linear models.

9. Write the zero-inflated likelihood yourself

Main point: You can write the zero-inflated Poisson likelihood with two terms: one term for y > 0 and one term for y = 0. Calculate the terms on the log scale with stable functions. This prevents rounding errors.

9.1 The likelihood with explicit mixtures

pm.ZeroInflatedPoisson calculates the likelihood formula from Section 8.2. It also prevents some types of numerical error. The code below gives the same model, but with the mixture written explicitly. pm.Potential adds a term to the log-probability of the model. PyMC uses the sum of all these terms to calculate the gradient for the sampler.

y_pos = y[y > 0]
n_zero = int(np.sum(y == 0))

with pm.Model() as m12_3_alt:
    ap = pm.Normal("ap", -1.5, 1)
    al = pm.Normal("al", 1, 0.5)
    log_p = pm.math.log(pm.math.invlogit(ap))          # log p
    log1m_p = pm.math.log(pm.math.invlogit(-ap))       # log(1 - p), stable
    lam = pm.math.exp(al)
    # y > 0: the monks worked AND finished y manuscripts
    lp_pos = log1m_p + pm.logp(pm.Poisson.dist(mu=lam), y_pos)
    # y = 0: the monks drank OR (worked AND finished nothing)
    lp_zero = pm.math.logaddexp(log_p, log1m_p - lam)
    pm.Potential("loglik", lp_pos.sum() + n_zero * lp_zero)
    idata_12_3_alt = pm.sample(1000, chains=4, random_seed=1)

az.summary(idata_12_3_alt, var_names=["ap", "al"], ci_prob=0.89).round(2)

The result is the same as for m12_3.

The two terms are:

  • For y > 0: log(1 − p) + log Pr(y | λ). This is the log of the probability that the monks work and complete y manuscripts.

  • For y = 0: log(p + (1 − p) exp(−λ)). This is the log of the probability that the monks drink or (work and complete nothing). Each zero has the same term, so the code multiplies it by the number of zeros.

9.2 Two tricks for numerical stability

The code uses two tricks:

  1. A stable log(1 − p). We need log(1 − p). If p is very near 1, then 1 − p can round to exactly 0. Then the log is negative infinity. The logit of 1 − p is −ap. Thus, log(invlogit(−ap)) gives log(1 − p) without this rounding error.

  2. A stable mixture of log-probabilities. The probability of a zero mixes two probabilities. logaddexp(a, b) calculates log(exp(a) + exp(b)). It uses methods that prevent rounding errors. Thus, logaddexp(log p, log(1 − p) − λ) is log(p + (1 − p) exp(−λ)), but it is more stable when p has extreme values.

The code below shows the two tricks:

ap_big = 40.0                          # log-odds, so p is almost exactly 1
print(np.log(1 - expit(ap_big)))       # 1 - p rounds to 0: the result is -inf
print(np.log(expit(-ap_big)))          # the same quantity, calculated safely: -40.0

# the probability of a zero, two ways
p, lam = 0.2, 1.0
print(np.log(p + (1 - p) * np.exp(-lam)))                  # direct
print(np.logaddexp(np.log(p), np.log(1 - p) - lam))        # stable, the same value

In this example, the stable method gives no difference. The direct method is satisfactory. But it is good to know the better method. More complex models do not work correctly without it.

Also, make sure that y contains integers when you write your own likelihood. The built-in PyMC distributions check the data type, but your own code does not.

10. Glossary

The terms are in alphabetical order. The links in the text go to these entries.

Aggregate

To put many observations into one row of the data, as one count. To disaggregate is to put each observation on its own row.

Beta distribution

A probability distribution for probabilities. It has values from 0 to 1. In this paper, its parameters are the mean p̄ and the shape θ.

Beta-binomial model

A model that gives each binomial count its own probability of success. These probabilities have a beta distribution.

Closed form

A formula that you can calculate directly, with no integral or other operation that stays open.

Continuous mixture

A distribution in which each observation has its own parameter value from a continuous distribution. The beta-binomial and the gamma-Poisson distributions are continuous mixtures.

Dispersion

Another name for the variance of a variable.

Gamma distribution

A continuous probability distribution for positive values. In a gamma-Poisson model, it gives the distribution of the Poisson rates.

Gamma-Poisson model

A model that gives each Poisson count its own rate. These rates have a gamma distribution. Its variance is λ + λ²/φ.

Generative process

The process that makes the data. You can simulate data from it.

Heterogeneity

Variation between cases, for example in probabilities or rates, that the predictor variables do not explain.

Link function

A function that connects a parameter of a likelihood to a linear model, for example the logit link or the log link.

Maximum entropy

A property of a distribution: it is the most spread-out distribution that agrees with the known constraints.

Mixture model

A model that uses more than one simple probability distribution for the same outcome variable, because the outcome has more than one cause.

Multilevel model

A model that estimates the residual of each observation (or group) and also the distribution of these residuals. Chapter 13 in the textbook explains these models.

Negative-binomial model

Another name for the gamma-Poisson model.

Over-dispersion

More variation in counts than a pure count model expects, after we condition on all the predictor variables.

Pareto k

A value from PSIS that shows how influential an observation is. A larger value shows a more influential observation.

Posterior validation check

A plot that compares the predictions of a fitted model with the raw data.

Shape parameter

A parameter that controls how spread out a distribution is. In a beta distribution, θ is the shape parameter.

Zero-inflated model

A mixture model that adds extra zeros to a count distribution. The extra zeros come from a binary process, for example a day of drinking.
