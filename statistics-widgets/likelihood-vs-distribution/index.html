Distribution or likelihood? — Binomial(x | n, p)

One expression, two jobs. Hold p fixed and vary the count: a probability distribution over x, discrete, summing to 1. Hold x fixed and vary the parameter: a likelihood for p, continuous, and not a distribution — its area is 1/(n+1).

Why it matters. The column is a function of p, not a distribution over p. Making it into one takes a prior — that is Bayes' rule.

What this shows

Binomial(x | n, p) takes two arguments, and which one you hold fixed changes what kind of object it is. The big panel shows the value at every combination: x runs across in discrete bands, one per possible count, and p runs continuously up the page. Darker means larger.

Cut it along a row, at one value of p, and you get the panel underneath: a probability distribution over the n+1 possible counts. It is discrete, so it is drawn as dots, and it sums to exactly 1.

Cut it down a column, at one value of x, and you get the panel on the right: the likelihood function for p. It is continuous, so it is drawn as a curve, and the area under it is 1/(n+1) — the same number for every x, and never 1. It is a perfectly good function of p, but it is not a probability distribution over p, and nothing about it is trying to be.

That is the distinction that matters when you compute a posterior. The likelihood does not tell you how plausible each p is; it tells you how well each p explains the data you saw. Turning that into a distribution over p takes a prior, and Bayes' rule.

How to use it

  • n — the number of trials, 1 to 100. It sets how many bands the surface has. At small n the discreteness of x is unmissable.
  • x — which column to cut. This is the data: the count you actually observed. The column, and the curve it produces, are marked in blue.
  • p — which row to cut. This is the parameter. The row, and the dots it produces, are marked in orange.
  • force the likelihood to area 1 — draws the column slice multiplied by n+1, dashed, so that its area becomes 1. Read the caption when you do.
  • Clicking or dragging on the surface moves both cuts at once.

Things to try

  • Drag x from 0 to n and watch the area reported under the right-hand panel. The curve changes shape completely — it slides across, it gets taller and narrower near the ends — and the area never moves off 1/(n+1). Nothing is normalising it; it just happens to integrate to that, for every x.
  • Now drag p and watch the sum reported under the bottom panel. It is 1 at every row, because that is what a distribution over x has to do.
  • Set x = 0. The likelihood is (1−p)n, largest at p = 0 and falling away. If it were a distribution over p, it would be saying p = 0 is the most probable value; as a likelihood it says only that p = 0 explains "no successes" better than anything else does. Those are different claims, and the second is the one the maths supports.
  • Turn on force the likelihood to area 1. The shape does not change at all — it is the same curve, multiplied by n+1. What you get is Beta(x+1, n−x+1), which is the posterior you would obtain from a uniform prior. So the rescaling that makes a likelihood into a distribution is not free: it is what Bayes' rule does, and it needs a prior to do it.
  • Raise n to 60 or so. The bands get thin and the surface starts to look continuous in both directions — but the bottom panel is still dots. There are only ever n+1 possible counts, however many that is.

Notes

  • Σx Binomial(x | n, p) = 1 is the binomial theorem. ∫01 Binomial(x | n, p) dp = C(n,x) B(x+1, n−x+1) = 1/(n+1) follows from the definition of the Beta function; the x-dependence cancels exactly. Both are reported live, and both are computed rather than asserted.
  • Shading is on a fixed scale so that the two readings stay comparable, with a gamma applied so mid-range values do not vanish. The corners, where p = 0 with x = 0 and p = 1 with x = n, both have value 1 and saturate.
  • The likelihood panel shares its vertical axis with the surface, so the curve lines up with the column it was cut from; the count panel shares its horizontal axis for the same reason. The dashed line marks p̂ = x/n, where the likelihood peaks.
  • Everything is computed in log space, so large n is no harder than small.
