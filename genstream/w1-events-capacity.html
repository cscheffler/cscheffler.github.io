Why the smaller group is the one that counts

Suppose 95 of your 100 alumni end up somewhere good. To learn what separates the two groups, every comparison you make leans on the five who didn't — they are the only contrast you have. A hundred people who all did well tell you almost nothing about why people do well.

So a programme can be genuinely excellent and still be unable to study itself. That is not a failure of analysis; it is arithmetic, and no statistical method gets around it. It is also the main reason this project recommends recording how far someone got rather than a yes/no — a graded outcome keeps the contrast that a yes/no throws away.

The last row does not mean anyone hands over their records

Nobody sends anyone a list of people. Each programme runs the same short calculation on its own data and shares only the totals that come out of it — sums and counts, no rows, nothing about any individual. Added together, those totals give the same answer as if all the records had been in one place. The bottom row is what that is worth.

The smaller programmes gain the most and risk the least: a programme with seventy alumni cannot answer any of these questions on its own, but its totals still count towards the answer — and it never publishes a figure of its own for anyone to pick apart.

If programmes can only share finished results instead

A simpler arrangement — everyone answers the questions alone and shares the answers — is easier to set up and gives noticeably less, because a programme can only contribute an answer to a question it was big enough to ask. The group is then limited by its smallest member:

What follows from this

  • Most programmes can ask about a handful of signals, not a selection process. At realistic numbers the typical row supports two or three questions at once. A real selection process uses ten or twenty signals, so most of it stays untested however carefully you analyse.
  • Working together is the only route to a real answer — and it costs no privacy. The bottom row needs nothing but totals: no records leave any programme, and no individual appears anywhere. That is the whole argument for a shared set of definitions, because totals can only be added up if everyone counted the same things the same way.
  • Which makes agreeing the columns the important decision. Not because a small programme cannot contribute — it can — but because a column only one programme collects cannot be added to anything.
  • A high success rate makes your data weaker, not stronger. Counterintuitive, and worth sitting with — it is the argument for measuring outcomes on a scale rather than as a yes/no.
  • You can find out where you stand today. Two numbers — how many alumni, and what share had a good outcome — put you on this chart. Neither requires anyone to share anything.
The arithmetic, and how much to trust it

Smaller group = alumni × whichever of (share good) or (1 − share good) is smaller. Questions you can ask ≈ smaller group ÷ data needed per question.

The "10 per question" convention comes from Peduzzi et al. (1996). It is a rule of thumb, not a law — it can be too lenient or too strict depending on how strong the signals are. The modern replacement computes a required sample directly: Riley et al. (2020), BMJ 368:m441. The slider is there so you can see that the conclusion here does not depend on picking 10.

The bottom row assumes each programme computes summary quantities on its own data — sums, counts and cross-products — and shares only those, which are added together and fitted centrally. For a linear model that gives an answer algebraically identical to fitting on the combined records; for the models used here it takes several rounds of the same exchange. The simpler alternative, where each programme fits the model alone and shares its conclusions, is what the collapsed table above describes; on the questions everyone can fit, the two agree closely — see Burke, Ensor & Riley (2017), Meta-analysis using individual participant data, Statistics in Medicine 36(5):855-875. What is lost is not precision but reach: a programme that could not fit the model contributes nothing at all.

Two caveats in the other direction. If programmes genuinely differ from one another, adding more of them sharpens the estimate of the average but never drives the uncertainty to zero, because part of it is real variation rather than noise. And more elaborate federated methods, which pass summaries back and forth over several rounds rather than once, can get closer to what combining the records would give — at the cost of shared infrastructure that does not exist yet. Programme figures come from programme-roster.csv. Its cohort-size column is flagged in programme-roster-schema.md as the roster's weakest, and several entries are marked UNVERIFIED. None of these numbers should be quoted as facts about a programme. They are here to show the size of the problem, and the size of the problem does not change if any single figure is off by half.
