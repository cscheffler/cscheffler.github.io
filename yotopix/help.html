Limits of the Yoto display

A Yoto icon is 16 × 16 pixels with no background. The display has no backlight behind a transparent pixel, so transparent is not a colour — it is the unlit panel. Draw the shape of the thing and leave everything around it empty. A filled square with a picture inside it will read as a glowing block.

The display also resolves each colour channel in steps of 15, so every colour here snaps to that grid. Two shades closer together than one step are the same shade on the device.

Tools

Down the left edge. Click one, or press its key.

Pen B

Click or drag to paint with the active colour. Drags are joined up, so a fast stroke does not come out as dots.

Eraser E

Makes pixels transparent again. You rarely need to select it: right-drag erases without switching tools, whatever tool you are holding.

Flood fill G

Fills the connected area under the pointer. Transparent counts as a colour, so this is also how you flood the empty space around a shape.

Eyedropper I

Picks the colour of a pixel. Hold Alt to borrow it without changing tools. If the colour belongs to a shading ramp, that ramp becomes the active one. Hold Shift to read the reference image instead of the drawing.

Line L

Drag from one end to the other. The line previews as you drag and lands when you let go.

Rectangle R

Drag out a corner-to-corner outline. Hold Shift to fill it instead.

Shade D

The unusual one. Instead of painting a colour, it moves each pixel you touch one step along its shading ramp — drag to lighten, hold Shift to darken. Sculpt light and shadow onto flat colour without picking shades by hand. A colour that is not already in the ramp joins it at its nearest step, so this works on flat artwork and not only on pixels you shaded yourself. Empty squares stay empty.

Drawing

  • Cmd/Ctrl+Z undoes, Cmd/Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes. History is per icon and lasts for the session.
  • Arrow keys shift the whole image by one pixel, wrapping round the edges. Useful for centring something you drew slightly off.
  • You can draw without a mouse: Shift+arrows move a caret around the grid and Space (or Enter) applies the current tool where it sits.
  • # turns the grid overlay on and off. Delete or Backspace clears the canvas, after asking.

Colour

The palette is a curated set that survives the display's steps of 15. Anything you choose elsewhere — the hex box, the colour picker, an imported photo — is snapped to the same grid, so what you see is what the device can actually show.

  • Custom slots hold eight colours of your own. Save to slot stores the active colour; 1–8 recall them.
  • Recent fills itself with what you have actually painted with.
  • X swaps back to the previous colour, for going to and fro between two.

Black is a special case

Black and transparent look identical on the display. A black pixel enclosed by lit pixels reads as a deliberate hole and is fine — an eye, a gap, an outline inside the shape. A black pixel that touches the outside silently becomes part of the background and changes the silhouette. Checks watches for exactly this.

Shading ramps

A ramp is a row of shades of one colour, from dark to light, generated for you: pick a base colour, choose how many steps, and say whether highlights should run warmer or cooler. Every shade is placed perceptually, so the steps look evenly spaced rather than merely being evenly numbered, and each one is snapped to the display's grid.

  • Click any swatch in a ramp to paint with it.
  • The Shade tool walks pixels up and down the active ramp.
  • [ and ] darken and lighten the single pixel under the pointer without changing tools — the quickest way to touch up one edge.

Ramps are saved with your gallery, so they are still there tomorrow.

Symmetry

Under the tools. Everything you draw is mirrored live, including flood fills and shading. M cycles through the modes.

Off

Draw normally.

Left to right

The usual one: faces, animals, anything front-on.

Top to bottom

Reflections and horizontal bands.

Both axes

Quarter the work; good for flowers and badges.

Eight-way

Also mirrors the diagonals, for snowflakes, stars and compass shapes.

Grid mode — the 15 × 15 trick

A 16-wide grid has no middle column, so a symmetrical drawing can never sit dead centre. Switching the Grid panel to one of the Odd 15 × 15 modes drops one row and one column, giving you a true centre line to mirror against. The anchor decides which edge is given up.

The dropped row and column are still exported — the file stays 16 × 16 — they are simply left empty, which the display shows as nothing. If you already drew something there, YotoPix says how many pixels are affected and offers to drop them — or to shift the whole image clear first, when there is somewhere for it to go.

Checks

A running list of things that will look wrong on the hardware but fine on your monitor. Most warnings come with a one-click fix that applies to every pixel at fault.

  • Art in the dropped row or column — in odd-grid mode, art that will not be exported.
  • Black touching the outside — will read as transparent and change the silhouette.
  • Pixels too dark to read — distinguishable on a bright screen, not on the panel.
  • Nothing drawn yet, and reaching all four edges — a design with no margin loses its silhouette against the dark panel.

The Device preview panel shows the icon small and unlit-dark, which catches things a 16×-magnified canvas hides.

Reference tracing

Load a picture underneath the grid and draw over it. It sits behind the checkerboard, so empty squares still look empty, and it is never part of your drawing or your export.

  • Opacity, scale and position are sliders; arrow keys nudge whichever one has focus.
  • ` shows and hides it while you work.
  • It is session-only — a photo is far too big to keep in the gallery, so closing the tab forgets it.

Picking colours off the reference

Hold Shift with the eyedropper to read the reference instead of your drawing (Alt+Shift to do it without putting the pen down). You always get the colour from the original image, so dimming it with the opacity slider changes what you see and not what you pick. Shift-pick samples chooses what gets read:

  • The whole square averages that entire grid square. Right for edges: a blurry or textured square resolves to the one colour it should be.
  • The exact spot reads a tiny patch under the pointer, for when a square straddles two colours and you want one of them.

Import an image

Import image under Export. Crop to a square, then watch the preview while you adjust:

  • Box average smooths detail down; nearest neighbour keeps hard edges. Box for photos, nearest for pixel art that is already blocky.
  • Alpha threshold decides how see-through a pixel must be before it becomes properly transparent — there is no half-transparent on this display.
  • Brightness and saturation push a washed-out photo into something that survives being 16 pixels wide.
  • Snap to palette colours matches every pixel to your palette.

Importing replaces the canvas in one step, so a single undo puts it back.

Paper to pixel

For drawing away from a screen, and the easiest way to hand this to a child.

  1. Open the printable grid and print it at 100%.
  2. Colour the squares in. One square is one pixel.
  3. Leave a square blank for transparent. White crayon will not come back — to the camera, and on the display, white paper and nothing are the same thing.
  4. Photograph the whole sheet, including the four corner marks, in even light.
  5. Paper to pixel in the editor: drag the four handles onto the corner marks and apply.

A slight angle is fine — that is what the four handles are for. Arrow keys nudge whichever handle has focus, which is easier than dragging for the last pixel or two.

Four controls sit beside the preview. Paper threshold decides how light a square has to be before it counts as blank paper — if the result comes back empty, the drawing is being read as paper, so lower it; if bare paper is coming through as colour, raise it. Correct the lighting white-balances against the paper itself, which is what stops an indoor photo importing as cream. Saturation pushes crayon back towards the colour it looked on the page, and Snap to palette colours matches the result to your palette. Very pale colours are the one thing that cannot be recovered.

Saving, exporting and the gallery

  • Your work saves itself to this browser as you draw. There is no save button and no server — nothing you make here leaves your machine.
  • Export PNG (Cmd/Ctrl+S) writes the 16 × 16 file to upload to Yoto.
  • The Gallery keeps every icon. Duplicate (Cmd/Ctrl+D) is the way to make a variant.
  • Back up writes a JSON file of everything — icons, ramps, custom slots and recents — and Restore reads one back, replacing what is in the gallery. Because the gallery lives in this browser, clearing site data would take it with it, so keep a backup somewhere real.
