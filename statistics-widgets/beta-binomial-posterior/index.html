From prior to posterior — prior × likelihood

The posterior is the prior reweighted by the likelihood: multiply the two at every value of p, then rescale by a constant. With a Beta prior and a Binomial likelihood the answer is another Beta, exactly.

What this shows

Bayes' rule for the globe-tossing golem of Chapter 2: a Beta prior on p, x successes in n trials, and the posterior that results.

p(p | x)  ∝  Binomial(x | n, p) × Beta(p | α, β)

Read it pointwise. At each value of p, take how plausible the prior finds that p, multiply by how well that p explains the data, and the result — once rescaled by a constant — is the posterior. Multiplication is the whole mechanism: a value of p survives only if both factors are large, so either one can veto it.

Because the Beta is conjugate to the Binomial, the answer is another Beta and the update is just bookkeeping: add the successes to α and the failures to β.

How to use it

  • α and β — the prior's shape, 0.1 to 50. Their total is how much the prior is worth in observations, so raising both makes the prior harder to move.
  • n and x — the data: x successes in n trials, with x held at or below n. At n = 0 there are no data, the likelihood is flat, and the posterior is exactly the prior.
  • What to show — all three overlays prior, likelihood and posterior on one axis, each scaled so its highest point is 1. Steps 1, 2 and 3 walk through the calculation instead, one curve at a time, on the true vertical scale.
  • p — a vertical line marking one value of p. In the overlay it reads off all three curves and does the multiplication for you.
  • Below the plot, the bar shows the posterior mean sitting between the prior mean and the observed proportion x/n, at a position set by their weights.

Things to try

  • At the defaults the prior leans to 0.5 and the data say 6/9 = 0.667. The posterior lands between them, at 0.615 — not at either. Move p to 0.3: the prior still gives it some support, but the likelihood is nearly zero there, so the product is nearly zero. That veto is what "reweighted by the likelihood" means.
  • Sweep p across the whole range and watch the last number in the readout, the one the product is divided by. It never changes. Normalising is a single constant, not something that reshapes the curve.
  • Click through steps 1, 2, 3. Between 2 and 3 the curve does not move at all — only the numbers on the vertical axis do. By step 2 you already have the answer; step 3 only decides what it is called.
  • Hold the data at 6/9 and raise α and β together: (1,1), (5,5), (30,30). Watch the marker on the bar slide back toward the prior mean as the prior outweighs nine observations. Then hold the prior and raise n instead, keeping x/n near 0.667 — the data take over.
  • Set α = 30, β = 3 (a prior sure that p is high) against x = 10, n = 100. Prior and likelihood barely overlap. In the overlay the posterior still sits between them; look at step 2 and see how small the product gets — its peak is about 10−18. The shape is still perfectly good; only the scale is tiny.

Notes

  • In the overlay, each curve is divided by its own highest value so that three quantities with quite different units share an axis. That is legitimate here because Bayes' rule fixes the posterior only up to a constant anyway. The posterior drawn is exactly the product of the two curves drawn, divided by that product's own peak — which is why the arithmetic in the readout lands on the curve.
  • The constant that turns the product into a probability density is ∫ Binomial(x | n, p) Beta(p | α, β) dp, the marginal likelihood — which is exactly the prior predictive probability of the x you observed, the quantity plotted in the prior-predictive widget. It rescales the curve and does nothing else, so nothing here depends on it.
  • The posterior is Beta(α+x, β+n−x), and its mean is an exact weighted average of the prior mean and x/n, with weights α+β and n. That is the sense in which a Beta(α, β) prior "is worth" α+β observations.
  • Densities are unbounded, and when α or β is below 1 the prior runs to infinity at an endpoint. Vertical scales are therefore taken from the central 98% of the range (p between 0.01 and 0.99), and anything above the top of the plot is clipped and marked with a caret. Everything is computed in log space with a Lanczos log Γ, so non-integer α and β are no harder than whole numbers.
