Methodology widgets

Small interactive explainers for the choices made in methodology.md (deliverable D1). Each one exists to convey why a decision matters and what the right intuition is — not to teach statistics.

Every widget names the section of methodology.md it serves. If it doesn't serve one, it's decoration and doesn't get built. Priorities and rationale live in methodology-widgets.md.
Presenting? present.html runs a chosen sequence of these as slides — PageDown and PageUp to move, widgets still fully interactive. Serve the repo over python3 -m http.server and edit the DECK list at the top of that file to re-sequence.

W1

How much can your data actually tell you?

Your sample size isn't your headcount — it's your smaller group. Shows why most individual programmes can't study their own selection process alone, why pooling is the method rather than a bonus, and why a very high success rate leaves you with less to learn from, not more.

Serves §1 Scope, §6 Analysis plan · feeds the case for a graded outcome in §4
W2

Why your own data shows nothing

Among your own fellows the work-test score barely predicts anything — which is what a selection process working looks like, not evidence the score is useless. Side-by-side scatter of everyone who applied against only those accepted, with the fix: keep the score distribution for the whole applicant pool.

Serves §7 Range restriction, §3.5
W4

What a yes/no outcome costs you

Two people who ended up in completely different places get recorded as the same thing. Shows how much of the signal a yes/no throws away, and why the damage is worst exactly where these programmes sit — with a line most alumni are already on the right side of. Works on a continuous outcome (months to placement) or on the ladder.

Serves §4 Outcome variables
W6

Keeping your existing headline number

Adopting a graded outcome doesn't cost you the single figure your board asks for. Crosswalks both a ladder and a continuous time-to-placement back to a yes/no headline — and shows that one cohort can honestly report anything from 26% to 92% depending only on where somebody drew the line.

Serves §4 Outcome variables, §10 Reporting

The reliability ceiling

If two reviewers don't agree with each other, there's a hard limit on what any signal can be shown to predict — however good the outcome measure is. The most actionable widget in the set.

W5

The same data, different answers

With a small sample and many signals, re-running the analysis on a slightly different slice of the same people changes which signals look important. Not a flaw in the analysis — a fact about the sample.
