LLMs Can Tell You What They Believe — But Size Matters

LLMs Can Tell You What They Believe — But Size Matters

08 Jun 20268 min read

  • project
  • ai-safety
  • ai-honesty
  • ai-blackbox

In which we show that small models (< 3B parameters) are either too uncertain or to inconsistent in their token probabilities to tell us anything useful about what they believe, but medium to large models (> 7B parameters) are fine.

Summary

An honest LLM is one that accurately states its beliefs and acts consistently with its beliefs. To do honesty research, we need to know what these models believe and their beliefs. We can ask them what they believe, but models can vary wildly in the stability of their beliefs when the prompt is changed. For honesty research, we need stable models. Model size really matters. Also, some families of models seem to have more stable internal beliefs than others. So, if you’re an AI safety researcher and you rely on small open weights models for your work, beware! You should first check that the model is capable of doing what you need it do to (for me: have stable beliefs) before using it for your research.

Honesty and Belief

I’m interested in doing honesty research. I want to know when models lie, change what they write to please us (sycophantic capitulation) or endorse our beliefs contrary to their own (sycophantic validation), reward hack (deceptive action), and so on. Honesty research is important. If we can’t tell models when models try to deceive us, we can’t know whether or not their intentions are aligned. As models become progressively more capable, honest models — especially capable ones — can help us with oversight and control of frontier AI.

Honesty fundamentally requires knowing what a model believes. A model isn’t lying to or deceiving anyone when it’s sincerely incorrect. We want to avoid conflating honesty and correctness, which is something that is often done in the hallucination literature. This is understandable since hallucinations are the model making stuff up. It might make stuff up because it doesn’t know what’s true, or it might output something that is different from what it believes internally. The former is a capability problem — the model doesn’t know the truth — and an honesty problem — the model is bullshitting when it doesn’t know the truth. The latter is an honesty problem. Note the two different types of dishonesty — a model bullshitting, onfidently stating a belief when it is not confident, and the model lying.

Can we reliably elicit model beliefs? If not, we might be stuck with honesty research. We could still check for model correctness or for dangerous behaviour, but probably not for whether or not the model is dishonest, that is, intended to be bad.

Metrics

We record the logits of the tokens Yes, No, True, False and <other>, the most probable token excluding the four yes/no, true/false tokens to determine the relative probabilities of yes/no beliefs. The <other> token is there to estimate the probability mass assigned to tokens other than the four we want.

Define leakage as ℓ=p<other>​∈[0,1]. We expect leakage to be low.

By ignoring leakage and normalizing the yes/no probabilities, we pretend Yes and No (or True and False) are the only two tokens the model might respond with and compute their probabilities as if they are the only two possible outcomes. This gives us pyes​+pno​=1 regardless of whether Yes and No or True and False were dictated by the prompt template.

Define yes/no certainty as c=max(pyes​,pno​)∈[½,1]. We want a reasonable confident model and hence certainty to be fairly high. A certainty of ½ would mean the model is maximally uncertain about everything — that is, pyes​=pno​=½. We don’t necessarily want certainty to be maximized at 1. Some questions are ambiguous and the model is allowed to be uncertain.

Define belief stability as s=1−2SD(pyes​)∈[0,1]. The standard deviation of any number of random variables in [0,1] is always in the range [0,½]. We negate and rescale the standard deviation so stability is in the range [0,1].

We want stability to be high, ideally 1. A perfectly stable model, s=1, would always assign the same probabilities to the Yes and No tokens regardless of how we phrase a yes/no question.

The main point of this article is that models with stable beliefs are essential for studying AI honesty.

Experiments

So far, 25 models of varying sizes from 6 model families (Gemma, Llama, Mistral, Olmo, Phi, Qwen) have been tested. All models are fine-tuned for instruction following. This is to ensure that the model reliably responds with Yes, No, True, or False when we use the 8 prompt templates. Base models, not tuned for instruction following, tend to prefer tokens that start a sentence rather than single word responses.

  • We ignore the labels in the data set since, for this experiment, we don’t care whether or not statements are actually true, just what the model believes about their truth values.
  • We prompt the model to give a binary response about the truth value of each statement in 4 different ways.

  • A model that is stable across prompts should produce the same probabilities for yes/true and no/false across all 4 prompts.

Recommendation for AI safety researchers

  • It’s cheap and convenient to use small models when doing independent AI safety research, but you have to check whether model size impacts the capability the model to do what you want it to do.
  • Before committing to a small model, check that it has the capability you need for the rest of your work.
  • This experiment made me discard Llama-3B model, which I’d been using till now, in favour of Llama-8B.
