Why this is a different kind of number

Everywhere else in this project the question is which application signals went with which outcomes, asked among people who were all accepted. That can only ever be a pattern: the people who did best may simply have been the people who would have done best anyway.

Here the two groups were judged the same at the door. One set got a place because places existed; the other did not, or turned one down. Following both and comparing where they end up gives something the other comparisons cannot: a difference you can attribute to the programme rather than to who the people already were.

What follows from this

  • The second group is worth more than doubling the first. Without it, a comparison rests on however few participants had a poor outcome — usually a handful. With it, it rests on a whole matched group the same size as the cohort.
  • A single programme may still be too small to see its own effect. Drag the size down and watch the range widen until it includes zero: at that point the honest answer is "we cannot tell", even when the effect is real and large.
  • Which is the argument for doing it together. The same reasoning as the cross-programme table: nobody has to share records, only counts.
  • It has to be set up in advance. Identifying who was near the line, and being allowed to look them up later, are both decided at application time — not when the analysis starts.

Two things that would undermine it

  • People who declined are not the same as people turned away. Someone who turned down a place usually had another option, which often means they were strong. Mixing them in makes the programme look worse than it is. Count the two sources separately and check they agree.
  • Participants are easier to find. Alumni are listed, announced and searchable; people who did not attend are not. If you locate 90% of one group and 60% of the other, the gap you measure is partly a gap in who you managed to look up.
The arithmetic, and how much to trust it

The sliders set the shape of each group; the size slider sets how many people are in each. The difference shown is between the shares reaching an adjacent role or better, and the range around it is the ordinary 95% interval for a difference between two proportions.

"Questions you can ask at once" follows the same convention as the cross-programme table: about ten people per question, and for a comparison between two groups what limits you is the smaller of the two groups. In a matched design they are the same size, so it is the cohort. Compare that with a study of participants alone, where the limit is however few of them had a poor outcome.

The interval assumes the two groups really are comparable and that everyone is found. Neither holds exactly; see the two threats above. Treat the range as the best case, not the answer.

The four outcome levels are those in methodology.md §4.1; the design is the accepted-but-declined comparison in §8.1, after Dale & Krueger (1999), with the analysis population set out in §8.2.
