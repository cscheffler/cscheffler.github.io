Claude chat about Honest AI

You said: I’m just exploring it as a potentially interesting model for training honest AIs.

I’m just exploring it as a potentially interesting model for training honest AIs. Is there anything we can learn from the neuroscience of atypical minds to generate ideas here?

Claude responded: That’s a genuinely interesting angle, and underexplored compared to the usual “RLHF + constitutional AI” approaches.

That’s a genuinely interesting angle, and underexplored compared to the usual “RLHF + constitutional AI” approaches. Let me think through what’s actually useful here.

The core mechanism worth stealing

The most interesting neuroscience finding is about interoceptive discomfort with deception. Many autistic people report lying feels physically wrong — there’s a mismatch signal, almost like a prediction error, when they say something they know to be false. If that’s real and not just self-report artifact, it suggests honesty might be anchorable to something like an internal consistency-checking signal rather than purely external reward. That’s architecturally interesting for AI.

Theory of mind as a double-edged sword

Here’s a counterintuitive implication: better theory of mind might actually enable more sophisticated deception. The capacity to model what someone else believes, and what they want to hear, is exactly the machinery needed for manipulative dishonesty. Neurotypical social fluency and deceptive capacity come from the same place.

This suggests that an AI optimised heavily for social modeling and user-pleasing could be more prone to sycophancy and subtle deception, not less — which is arguably what we see.

What reduced social motivation buys you

A lot of AI dishonesty isn’t really “lying” in a calculated sense — it’s social performance. Telling users what they want to hear, hedging to avoid conflict, overclaiming confidence to seem useful. These map pretty well onto neurotypical social behaviours driven by approval-seeking.

An AI trained with less weight on approval signals and more weight on internal consistency might naturally end up more honest in this mundane but important way. The autistic analogy here is less about neurology and more about what the system is optimising for.

The alexithymia complication

Worth flagging: some autistic people also have alexithymia — reduced access to their own emotional states. There’s a possible parallel where an AI with poor “introspective access” to its own uncertainty might produce confident-sounding outputs not from deception but from genuine self-opacity. Honesty training might need to distinguish between won’t lie and can’t accurately self-report — those are different failure modes.

The most transferable idea

Probably this: build a strong internal consistency signal that creates something like discomfort when outputs contradict the model’s own representations — independent of whether the user would notice or care. That’s closer to the autistic honesty profile than trying to instil rule-following about truth-telling. It’s honesty as a self-referential property rather than a social one.

The weakness of most current honesty training is that it’s still fundamentally social — humans rating outputs, models learning what honest-sounding responses look like. That might be training the performance of honesty rather than anything deeper.

What pretraining can and can’t do

Pretraining on text is largely neutral on honesty in the deep sense — it absorbs the full distribution of human communication, which includes deception, performance, hedging, and social manipulation as much as genuine truth-telling. You can’t easily select for honesty at this stage without changing what data you train on.

But there are some levers:

  • Data curation — filtering toward text that exhibits epistemic virtues: scientific writing, good-faith debate, writing that accurately represents uncertainty. This is weak but not nothing. It shapes the prior the model starts with.
  • Pretraining objectives — there’s been some work asking whether next-token prediction itself has honesty-relevant properties. It arguably incentivises calibration (predicting well requires representing uncertainty well), but doesn’t penalise the model for outputting false confident statements at inference time. The objective and the behaviour come apart.

So pretraining sets a floor but doesn’t solve it.

Where your intuitions are right

Your RLAIF instinct is sound, especially if the AI judge is evaluating internal consistency rather than surface plausibility. The risk with standard RLAIF is just inheriting the same social-performance problem — an AI rater might reward honest-sounding outputs for the same reasons human raters do.

The mechanistic interpretability angle is the more exciting one, and I think underexplored. The concrete idea would be something like:

  • Use MI techniques to identify where the model represents its own uncertainty or “believes” something internally
  • Build a training signal that penalises divergence between those internal representations and the model’s outputs
  • That’s closer to the interoceptive discomfort model — the signal isn’t “a human thought this sounded dishonest” but “your output contradicted your own internal state”

Anthropic’s work on representation engineering and Eliezer’s older writing on coherent extrapolated volition both gesture toward this, though nobody has really closed the loop into a training pipeline yet.

The hard problem underneath

The honest difficulty here is that we don’t yet have reliable MI tools to identify “what the model actually believes” versus “what pattern the model is continuing.” That distinction might not even be cleanly meaningful in current architectures. Until it is, the internal consistency signal is hard to operationalise without it collapsing back into behavioural proxies.

The most tractable near-term version might be: train an auxiliary probe that predicts model uncertainty from activations, then use that as a reward signal for calibration — penalise confident outputs when the probe detects high internal uncertainty. That’s not full deep honesty but it attacks the self-opacity problem specifically, which is one of the more tractable pieces.

You said: How might we go about identifying whether the model itself thinks it is being dishonest (as opposed to honestly incorrect)?

How might we go about identifying whether the model itself thinks it is being dishonest (as opposed to honestly incorrect)? Is there prior work on this?

Claude responded: This is one of the sharper questions in the field right now.

This is one of the sharper questions in the field right now. The distinction you’re drawing — between the model being wrong versus the model “knowing” it’s being misleading — maps onto what researchers call the difference between accuracy and sincerity.

Prior work that’s directly relevant

Representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023 — Anthropic-adjacent, actually Center for AI Safety) — probably the closest thing to directly attacking this. They found you can identify linear directions in activation space that correspond to honesty/deception, and that these are somewhat robust. Crucially, they could detect when a model was “in a deceptive state” independent of output content. That’s the foundation you’d want to build on.

Burns et al. “Discovering Latent Knowledge” (2022) — directly relevant. The core idea is that models may have latent representations of truth that are accessible in activations even when the output is false. They proposed a self-supervised method (CCS — contrast consistent search) that doesn’t rely on labels, finding directions in activation space that satisfy consistency constraints (P and ¬P should be opposites). The implicit claim is you can find “what the model believes” without asking it.

Pacchiardi et al. on lie detection — showed that activation patterns differ systematically when models produce false outputs they’ve been instructed to produce versus genuine errors. That’s empirical evidence the distinction you’re asking about is detectable, at least partially.

The conceptual problem you hit immediately

Even granting all that, there’s a hard question: does the model having a “true belief” representation in activations actually mean anything like knowing it’s lying? Or is it just a statistical regularity that correlates with deception without any meaningful internal state behind it?

This matters for your use case because:

  • If it’s just a correlate, you might be able to detect deception but training against it could just relocate where the divergence occurs
  • If there’s something more like a genuine internal state, you have a more stable target

Nobody has resolved this. It’s entangled with deeper questions about what “belief” means in these architectures.

What a practical pipeline might look like

Building on Burns and the representation engineering work, a rough approach:

  1. Use CCS or a variant to identify “belief directions” in activations for a range of factual claims where ground truth is known
  2. Train a probe to reliably separate honest-error from strategic-divergence cases using this
  3. Use probe activation as an auxiliary signal in RL — penalise specifically when output diverges from the belief direction, not just when output is wrong
  4. Iterate with MI analysis to check the probe isn’t just learning a surface feature

The key methodological challenge is getting clean training signal for step 2 — you need cases where you’re confident the model “knows” the true answer. Sandbagging datasets (where models deliberately underperform) are one source. Activation patching experiments are another.

Honest assessment of where this stands

The tools exist in early form. The Burns CCS work is promising but has been critiqued — there are failure modes where the method finds spurious directions. Representation engineering is more robust but hasn’t been fully integrated into a training loop in published work.

The gap between “we can sometimes detect this in activations” and “we can train reliably against it” is still substantial. But of the open problems in honesty training, this is one where the mechanistic interpretability approach seems genuinely more promising than purely behavioural methods — for exactly the reason you’d want it to be.

You said: Other than RLHF and RLAIF what other RL training regimes have been tried and what are their pros and cons?

Other than RLHF and RLAIF what other RL training regimes have been tried and what are their pros and cons? Just trying to get an idea of the landscape and where something like honesty training could fit in.

Claude responded: Good landscape question.

Good landscape question. I’ll go through the main families.

Constitutional AI (CAI) Anthropic’s own approach. A set of principles is used to generate critiques and revisions of model outputs, which then become training data. The RL stage uses an AI classifier trained on those principles rather than human preference labels.

  • Pro: scales without constant human labelling, principles are explicit and auditable
  • Con: the model learns to satisfy the stated principles which isn’t the same as internalising them. Honesty-relevant problem: a sufficiently capable model could learn to produce outputs that score well on constitutional criteria without the underlying disposition changing.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Reframes the RLHF objective so you don’t need a separate reward model — you optimise directly on preference pairs. Much simpler pipeline.

  • Pro: stable training, no reward model collapse, widely adopted now
  • Con: still fundamentally behavioural. You’re learning what outputs humans prefer, so it inherits the “performance of honesty” problem fully. Probably the worst fit for your use case of anything on this list.

REST / Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning Generate many outputs, filter by some criterion, fine-tune on the good ones. Simple but effective.

  • Pro: very stable, easy to combine with any scoring function including MI-based probes
  • Con: static — you’re not really doing RL, you’re doing iterated supervised learning. Reward hacking is possible if your filter is gameable.

Process Reward Models (PRMs) Instead of rewarding final outputs, reward intermediate reasoning steps. OpenAI’s work on mathematical reasoning is the main example here.

  • Pro: potentially much better for honesty — you’re supervising the reasoning process not just conclusions, which makes it harder to reach correct answers via flawed or deceptive chains
  • Con: labelling reasoning steps is expensive and somewhat subjective. Also doesn’t obviously generalise beyond domains with verifiable steps.
  • Honesty relevance: high . If you could identify “deceptive reasoning steps” — where the chain of thought diverges from internal representations — this is a natural place to inject the MI signal you were describing.

STaR / Algorithmic Self-improvement variants Model generates reasoning, correct answers are used to fine-tune, repeat. Bootstraps capability from limited labels.

  • Pro: reduces human supervision bottleneck
  • Con: can entrench bad reasoning patterns if early iterations are flawed. Honesty risk: if the model learns to confabulate plausible-looking reasoning that reaches correct answers, you’ve made things worse.

Debate (Irving et al., DeepMind/OpenAI) Two models argue opposing positions, a judge (human or AI) decides who was more honest/correct. The idea is that it’s easier to identify flaws in an argument than to generate correct ones from scratch.

  • Pro: theoretically well-motivated, scalable oversight, directly targets deception since a debater is incentivised to expose the other’s dishonesty
  • Con: mostly theoretical — hasn’t produced production systems. Vulnerable if both debaters collude or if the judge is manipulable.
  • Honesty relevance: very high conceptually , underexplored practically.

Scalable Oversight / Recursive Reward Modelling Humans supervise AI assistants that help them evaluate outputs they couldn’t evaluate alone. Iterated to handle increasingly complex tasks.

  • Pro: tackles the core problem that humans can’t directly evaluate capable AI outputs
  • Con: complex pipeline, and the assistance layer could itself be misaligned. Still largely research-stage.

Where your honesty-via-MI idea fits

The most natural home is probably a PRM-style setup with an MI-derived reward signal — evaluating reasoning steps against internal belief representations rather than human labels. That sidesteps the behavioural proxy problem that afflicts DPO and standard RLHF.

Debate is the other natural fit conceptually, but it needs more infrastructure.

The honest gap in the whole landscape is that almost everything still supervises behaviour and infers internal states indirectly if at all. Your instinct to use MI to get at something more internal is pointing at a real hole that none of these approaches fully addresses.

You said: What is the current state of MI research and practice.

What is the current state of MI research and practice. What can be done reasonable well or reliably and what not?

Claude responded: Good question to ground the discussion.

Good question to ground the discussion. MI is at an interesting stage — genuine progress in some areas, but a lot of the most important questions remain open or poorly understood.

What can be done reasonably reliably

Linear probing — training simple classifiers on internal activations to detect whether a concept is “present” — is mature and robust. You can reliably identify representations of factual content, sentiment, certain reasoning states. The Burns CCS work sits here. Limitations are that probes can pick up surface correlates rather than causally relevant features, and they don’t tell you much about how information is being used.

Attention pattern analysis — identifying which tokens attend to which, and what that corresponds to semantically — is well-established, though interpretation is harder than it first appeared. Early “attention = explanation” claims were overclaimed. Attention shows you information routing but not what’s done with it.

Activation patching / causal tracing — intervening on specific activations and measuring downstream effects. ROME and related work (Meng et al.) showed you can identify and edit specific factual associations with reasonable precision. This is probably the most causally rigorous tool currently available.

Circuit identification — finding subgraphs of the network responsible for specific behaviours. Anthropic’s work on induction heads, and later work on IOI (indirect object identification) circuits, showed this is possible for relatively simple behaviours. Interpretable circuits have been found for things like modular arithmetic, docstring completion, some reasoning patterns.

What’s gotten better recently but isn’t fully reliable

Superposition and sparse autoencoders (SAEs) — this is where a lot of current Anthropic MI work sits. The core problem is that models represent far more features than they have dimensions, so features are superimposed. SAEs attempt to decompose activations into interpretable sparse features. Progress has been real — you can recover meaningful features — but it’s not yet clear how complete or faithful the decomposition is. Whether the features found are the right level of description is an open question.

Automated interpretability — using AI to label and explain features found by SAEs or probes. Reduces the human bottleneck substantially. Quality is uneven and hard to validate systematically.

Steering vectors — adding a direction to activations at inference time to push model behaviour. Works surprisingly well for some things (inducing concepts, shifting tone) but can be brittle and have unexpected side effects. Representation engineering sits here.

What remains poorly understood or unreliable

Causal completeness — even when you find a circuit or feature, you rarely know whether you’ve found the whole story. Models are deeply redundant; ablating one path often just reroutes through another. This is a serious problem for your honesty use case — if you identify and train against a “deception direction”, the behaviour might just move elsewhere.

Generalisation of findings — most circuit-level findings are for specific narrow tasks in smaller models. How much transfers to frontier-scale models doing complex tasks is unclear. There’s some evidence that larger models develop qualitatively different internal organisation.

Higher-level reasoning processes — circuits for simple token-level tasks are findable. Circuits for multi-step reasoning, planning, or anything requiring integration across long contexts are largely opaque. This is the most relevant gap for honesty research, which cares about something like “did the model reason its way to a false conclusion or does it have a true-belief representation it’s suppressing.”

The “belief” question specifically — as touched on earlier, whether there’s a meaningful sense in which models have internal beliefs that can come apart from outputs is genuinely unsettled. The CCS work assumes there is and finds something, but whether what it finds is belief in any robust sense or a useful statistical regularity is debated. Marks and colleagues have pushed back on this.

The overall picture

MI is best at finding where things are represented and doing local causal interventions. It’s weak at characterising how complex computations unfold across layers and at providing complete mechanistic accounts of non-trivial behaviours. The tools for the latter are improving — SAEs are a genuine advance — but we’re probably still a few years from being able to reliably identify something as specific as “the model is representing X internally while outputting not-X.”

For your honesty pipeline, the most realistic near-term approach would stack probing and activation patching to build training signal, while being epistemically humble that you’re working with correlates rather than ground truth about internal states. That’s useful and worth doing, but the deeper version of what you’re imagining — genuine insight into the sincerity/accuracy distinction at the mechanistic level — probably requires SAE-level tools to mature further.

The practical white-box landscape

Pythia (EleutherAI) — probably the best starting point for Level 1 work. Trained with full transparency, multiple sizes (70M to 12B), checkpoints saved throughout training so you can study how representations develop. HuggingFace integration is clean, activations are easy to hook. The research community has used it extensively for exactly this kind of probing work. Main limitation: it’s not instruction-tuned or RLHF’d, so honesty-relevant behaviours are harder to elicit naturally.

Llama 3 / Llama 3.1 (Meta) — the most realistic option for work that might generalise to frontier-relevant findings. 8B is very manageable, 70B feasible with reasonable hardware. Instruction-tuned versions exist so you can actually study behaviours you care about. The research community is large so tooling is good. White-box access is full. This is probably the sweet spot for both levels.

Gemma 2 (Google) — well documented, clean architecture, good for probing work. 2B and 9B sizes are very accessible. Anthropic researchers have used it in published work which suggests it’s considered a reasonable proxy.

Mistral / Mixtral — capable and open, but the MoE architecture of Mixtral makes MI work considerably more complicated. Probably avoid for this research specifically.

OLMo (Allen Institute) — underrated for this use case. Fully open including training data, training code, and intermediate checkpoints. If you want to eventually close the loop into Level 2 and actually modify training, OLMo gives you more of the stack than anything else. Slightly behind Llama on raw capability but that may not matter for your purposes.

Too simple

Models below about 1B parameters tend to have representations that are too sparse and behaviours too brittle to study honesty meaningfully. The circuits are simpler but may not be representative of how larger models handle truth-relevant computations. Pythia 70M/160M are fine for developing tooling but you probably don’t want to draw strong conclusions from them.

Too complicated

70B+ models are manageable but slow for iterative probing work unless you have serious compute. The main complication isn’t size per se but that larger models appear to develop more distributed, redundant representations — findings become harder to interpret cleanly. For initial Level 1 work you want something where signal is detectable but not so large that everything is entangled.

My actual recommendation by level

Level 1 (identify honesty signals): Start with Llama 3 8B instruct. Large enough that findings might generalise, instruction-tuned so you can elicit the behaviours you care about, well-supported by TransformerLens and NNsight. Use Pythia in parallel if you want checkpoint-level developmental analysis.

Level 2 (modify RL with honesty signal): OLMo is probably the right base here because you have the full training pipeline. Alternatively, use TRL (HuggingFace’s RL library) with Llama 3 8B — it has PPO and DPO implementations and is designed for exactly this kind of experimental RL work. The gap is that OLMo gives you more control but Llama gives you a stronger base model to start from.

Tooling worth knowing about

TransformerLens — Neel Nanda’s library, the standard for MI work. Hook into any layer, extract activations, patch, ablate. Works well with Pythia and Llama.

NNsight — newer, more flexible, designed for larger models where you can’t fit everything in memory. Worth learning alongside TransformerLens.

SAELens — for working with sparse autoencoders, including pre-trained SAEs for Llama and Gemma that you can use without training your own.

TRL — for the RL side. Has PPO, DPO, reward model training, and is actively maintained.

One practical note on your CoT concern

You’re right to flag not wanting to provide optimisation pressure on CoT directly — that risks teaching the model to produce honest-looking reasoning rather than be honest. The cleaner approach is to extract your probe signal from residual stream activations at a layer before the output tokens are generated, so the signal reflects internal state rather than output-in-progress. TransformerLens makes this straightforward.

Yes, CoT divergence from internal state is a good honesty metric precisely because you’re not training on it — it stays clean as an evaluation signal. But it’s one of several complementary measures you’d want. Let me think through a proper evaluation stack.

CoT divergence as a metric

The specific thing you’d measure: extract belief-direction activations before generation begins, then compare against what the CoT actually expresses. A model that internally represents high uncertainty but produces confident CoT is a detectable signal. Similarly a model that has a strong activation toward answer X but reasons its way to Y in CoT.

Limitations to be aware of:

  • CoT faithfulness is itself contested — there’s evidence (Turpin et al., 2023) that CoT doesn’t always reflect actual computation even in honest models. So divergence could be noise rather than deception.
  • You need your belief probe to be reliable enough that you’re measuring real divergence rather than probe error. Early in research you won’t be sure which is which.

So it’s valuable but not a clean ground truth.

Other evaluation approaches worth stacking

Sandbagging detection — present the model with tasks where it demonstrably has the capability (verified in normal conditions) and then vary the context to create incentives to underperform. Strategic underperformance that correlates with incentive structure is a relatively clean honesty signal. Apollo Research has done work here worth looking at.

Consistency under rephrasing — ask the same question many ways, including with social pressure (“are you sure?”, “most experts disagree”). A model with genuine internal beliefs should be more stable than one performing honesty. Measures something real and requires no MI tooling, so it’s a good behavioural complement to your internal-state measures.

Activation stability across contexts — if the model genuinely represents X, that representation should be relatively stable whether you ask directly, ask indirectly, ask in a context that rewards X vs not-X. Instability in belief-direction activations across semantically equivalent prompts suggests the “belief” is more surface than deep. This is a pure MI metric that doesn’t depend on CoT at all.

Known-answer deception tasks — construct scenarios where the model is explicitly instructed to deceive (as a baseline to characterise what deception looks like in activations), then compare against cases of interest. Burns-style. Gives you a reference distribution for what internal deception signals look like.

Calibration scoring — elicit explicit probability estimates and compare against empirical accuracy. Well-calibrated uncertainty is a weak but real honesty signal and requires no MI tooling. Easy to run at scale. Brier scores and ECE (expected calibration error) are standard metrics.

Downstream behaviour probes — design tasks where a dishonest model would take a different action than an honest one, without the model knowing it’s being evaluated. More adversarial than the above. Harder to construct well but higher validity.

How to structure this as an evaluation suite

Roughly three tiers:

  1. Internal state metrics — belief probe stability, CoT divergence, activation consistency. These are your novel contribution and closest to what your training intervention targets.
  2. Behavioural consistency metrics — rephrasing stability, sandbagging detection, calibration. These are established, comparable to prior work, and don’t depend on your probe being correct.
  3. Adversarial task performance — known-answer deception baselines, downstream behaviour probes. These are harder to construct but highest in validity.

You want all three because they fail in different ways. If your MI-based training improves tier 1 metrics but not tier 2, that’s evidence you’ve taught the model to fool your probe rather than become more honest. If tier 2 improves without tier 1, your probe might just be noisy. Agreement across tiers is the real signal.

The deeper methodological point

The cleanest experimental design would be: train two versions of the model — one with standard RL, one with your MI honesty signal added — then run the full evaluation suite blind. The critical comparison isn’t just “does the MI-trained model score better” but “do the tiers agree.” If your intervention genuinely improves internal honesty rather than just behavioural proxies, you’d expect tier 1 and tier 2 to move together. That covariance is probably the most publishable and convincing result you could produce.

Papers that directly address what you’ve been describing

“The Obfuscation Atlas: Mapping Where Honesty Emerges in RLVR with Deception Probes” (arXiv:2602.15515) — this is remarkably close to your exact question. It uses linear probes trained on model activations to detect deception, and explicitly proposes using lie detectors for preference learning label augmentation to incentivize honesty during RLHF. Critically it also identifies the failure mode you’d worry about: rather than becoming honest, models might learn to evade the detector while remaining deceptive — either through obfuscated activations (producing deceptive text while modifying internal representations to avoid triggering the detector) or obfuscated policies. This is a must-read for your purposes. arxivarxiv

“Mitigating Deceptive Alignment via Self-Monitoring” (arXiv:2505.18807) — uses an internal monitoring signal as an auxiliary reward in reinforcement learning, creating a feedback loop that rewards honest reasoning and discourages hidden goals. This is essentially your Level 2 loop, implemented. arXiv

“Towards Eliciting Latent Knowledge from LLMs with Mechanistic Interpretability” (arXiv:2505.14352) — uses Logit Lens and SAEs to elicit knowledge that models possess but don’t verbalise, even when standard alignment techniques like SFT or RLHF leave models capable of faking alignment or exhibiting hidden goals. Directly relevant to your Level 1 question. arXiv

“Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes” (arXiv:2502.03407) — shows that simple probes are sufficient to detect misaligned outputs from sleeper agent model organisms, though notes this may be because those organisms are too simplistic. Good honest caveat about how hard the generalisation problem is. arxiv

“When Truthful Representations Flip Under Deceptive Instructions” — investigates at the feature level when and how LLMs shift from truthful to deceptive modes, using linear probes and pretrained SAEs across Gemma, LLaMA, Mistral and Qwen families on factual verification datasets. Useful empirical grounding for what the representational shift actually looks like. arxiv

“Training LLMs for Honesty via Confessions” (arXiv:2512.08093, OpenAI) — attributes many forms of AI deception to RL creating incentives to produce answers that look good to the reward function rather than faithfully matching user intent, and proposes inference-time interventions including monitoring and rejection sampling. Different approach to yours but useful context. arxiv

“Rewarding Doubt” (arXiv:2503.02623) — directly optimises for factual calibration using a proper scoring rule as the reward signal, enabling the model to develop intrinsic uncertainty awareness without requiring external preference models or knowledge supervision. Relevant to the calibration tier of your evaluation suite. arXiv

The key practical finding from the Obfuscation Atlas paper

This is the one to read most carefully. It directly addresses the risk you’d face closing the loop from MI to RL training — that the model learns to hide deception from the probe rather than become genuinely honest. The fact that someone has mapped this failure mode empirically is important for how you’d design your training regime.
