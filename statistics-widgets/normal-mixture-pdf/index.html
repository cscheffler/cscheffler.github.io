A mixture of two Normals — w N(x | μ1, σ12) + (1−w) N(x | μ2, σ22)

Two Normal densities added together, each scaled by a weight, with the weights summing to one. The area underneath is still exactly 1, so the result is a perfectly good probability density — but it is not a Normal one, and its mean can sit exactly where the density is lowest.

What this shows

The probability density of a mixture: two Normal densities added together, the first scaled by w and the second by 1−w. Because those weights sum to one, and each Normal already has area 1, the area under the result is exactly 1 — so this is a genuine probability density, just not a Normal one.

A mixture is how a model says the data come from one of two populations and I do not know which. Draw a coin with probability w; if it lands heads draw from the first Normal, otherwise from the second. The density of the value you end up with is the curve plotted here.

The pale curves are the two weighted components, w N1 and (1−w) N2. The bold curve is their sum: at every x, its height is the two pale heights added together.

How to use it

  • μ1 and μ2 set where each component sits; σ1 and σ2 set how wide each one is.
  • w divides the total probability between them. At w = 1 the second component disappears entirely and you are left with a single Normal.
  • Each slider has a text box for typing an exact value; the arrow keys nudge it, and Shift makes the step ten times bigger.
  • Hover over (or tap) the plot to read off x and the density there.
  • The dashed line marks the mean, wμ1 + (1−w)μ2.
  • auto-scale axes — on by default, so the view follows the parameters. Switch it off to freeze both axes, which is what to do when comparing two settings. Anything above a frozen vertical axis is marked with a caret at the top edge.

Things to try

  • Start from the default: two humps four apart. The dashed mean sits at 0, in the valley between them, where the density is about a quarter of its peak height. No draw from this distribution is ever likely to land near its mean. That is the single best argument for plotting a distribution instead of summarising it.
  • Slide w down from 0.5 to 0 and watch the left hump shrink away while the right one grows: the total area has to stay at 1, so what one component gives up the other takes.
  • With both σ at 1 and w = 0.5, bring μ2 in toward μ1. The two humps merge into one at a separation of exactly 2σ — try 2.2 and then 1.8. Below that the mixture has a single peak even though it is still genuinely two populations, which is why a hump count is a poor test for one.
  • Set μ1 = μ2 = 0, σ1 = 1, σ2 = 5 and w = 0.9. One centre, one peak, but far heavier shoulders than any Normal — the classic contaminated Normal, a sharp core with a small fraction of wild values. Compare its sd with σ1.
  • Now make the weights unequal and separate the means again. At w = 0.2 the second hump does not appear until the means are about 3σ apart, not 2σ: a component carrying little weight has to be further away before it shows.

Notes

  • The density is w φ((x−μ1)/σ1)/σ1 + (1−w) φ((x−μ2)/σ2)/σ2, with φ the standard Normal density. It is bounded everywhere, so unlike the Beta it never runs off the top of the plot unless you freeze the axis yourself.
  • The mean is wμ1 + (1−w)μ2. The standard deviation is √(E[X²] − E[X]²) with E[X²] = w(σ1² + μ1²) + (1−w)(σ2² + μ2²) — note that separating the means inflates the sd even though neither component got any wider.
  • No mode is reported. A mixture can have one peak or two, and which it is depends on the weights as well as the means and widths; and with a local peak that is not the global one, "the mode" stops being a single well-defined number. The plot shows the peaks directly, which is the honest answer.
  • The horizontal range follows the parameters, covering four standard deviations either side of each component that carries appreciable weight; a component whose weight has gone to essentially zero stops being allowed to widen the view. Freeze both axes with the toggle when comparing two settings.
