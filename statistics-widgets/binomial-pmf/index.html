What this shows

The probability mass function of the Binomial distribution, Binomial(k | n, p): the probability of observing exactly k successes out of n independent trials when each trial succeeds with probability p. It is the likelihood function you reach for whenever the data are counts of successes out of a known number of tries — the globe-tossing example from Chapter 2 is exactly this.

Because k can only take the whole-number values 0, 1, …, n, the distribution is discrete. Each dot is a probability of its own, not a point on a curve, and all the dots together sum to 1.

How to use it

  • n — the number of trials. Raising it adds more possible outcomes, so the dots spread out and each individual probability gets smaller.
  • p — the probability of success on a single trial. It slides the bulk of the distribution left and right; the distribution is symmetric only at p = 0.5.
  • Each slider has a text box beside it for typing an exact value; the arrow keys nudge it, and Shift makes the step ten times bigger. n runs from 1 to 200, p from 0 to 1.
  • Hover over (or tap) the plot to read off k and its exact probability.
  • auto-scale vertical axis — on by default, so the tallest dot always fills the plot. Switch it off to freeze the axis at its current maximum, and the dots then shrink or grow against a fixed scale as you change n and p. Anything taller than a frozen axis is marked with a small caret at the top edge; hovering still reads its true value.

Things to try

  • Set n = 9, p = 0.7 — the globe-tossing data. Which value of k is most probable, and how much probability does the observed k = 6 actually get?
  • Hold p = 0.5 and push n from 1 up to 100. The distribution keeps its shape but grows wider in absolute terms — and narrower as a fraction of n. That gap is the law of large numbers.
  • Set p = 0.05 with n = 100. The distribution piles up against zero and turns strongly asymmetric — a normal approximation would be a poor golem here.
  • Push p to exactly 0 or 1. All the probability collapses onto a single outcome: with no uncertainty in a trial, there is none in the count.

Notes

  • Probabilities are computed in log space — log n! − log k! − log(n−k)! + k log p + (n−k) log(1−p) — and exponentiated at the end, so the binomial coefficient never overflows, even at the largest n on offer.
  • The dots are joined to the axis by thin stems purely to make them easier to read. The probability is the dot; the stem carries no meaning.
  • The URL updates as you change the inputs, so you can copy it from the address bar and share a particular n and p with someone else. A frozen axis travels with the link too, as &y=, so everyone opening it sees the same scale.
