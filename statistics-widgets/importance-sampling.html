Importance Sampling Lab

    Experiments

    1. Uniform or curved? With One peak, make 2,000 samples with Uniform, then 2,000 with Curved. Which estimate gets near the true mean faster? Then look at the comparison card.
    2. Too narrow. With One peak, choose Narrow. Make 20,000 samples. Why is the estimate too low most of the time? What happens when a sample lands far from 3?
    3. Zero variance. Choose Ideal ∝ x·g(x). Look at the orange contributions. Why do they all have the same value?
    4. Unknown constant. Choose “Only H = Z·g”. Compare the standard estimate with the self-normalized estimate. What is the mean weight close to?
    5. In the wrong place. With One peak, choose Shifted. Where does f put most of its samples? Look at the weights on the left side of the middle panel. What happens to the estimate when a sample lands there?
    6. Beat Curved. With One peak, use the two sliders to make your own f. Look at the comparison card with N = 100. Can you get a smaller SD than Curved? How close can you get to the Ideal row?
    7. A missing peak. Choose Two peaks and Left only. Make 5,000 samples. The estimate settles, but far from the true mean. Which numbers in the Results card warn you? Now also choose “Only H = Z·g”. Is there still a warning?
    8. Cover both peaks. With Two peaks, compare Uniform, Wide and Mixture in the comparison card. Which f is best? Why can one normal f never be as good as Mixture here?

    Compare importance distributions

    Samples in each run (N)

    Each row does the whole estimate 300 times, with N samples each time. Each dot is one estimate. The green line is the true mean. A narrow cloud means that the estimate changes little from run to run, so that f is efficient. SD is the spread of the 300 estimates. Bias is their average minus the true mean. A good f has a small SD and a bias near 0. A small SD alone is not enough: an f can give the same wrong answer every time.
