What is going on

The people you accepted all scored well — that is what accepting them meant. So among your fellows there is very little difference in score left to compare against anything. A relationship needs variation at both ends to be visible, and your selection process removed the variation at one end on purpose.

Drag the slider to 100% and the slope comes back, because at that point you are looking at everyone. Nothing about the underlying relationship changed as you dragged. Only how much of it you were allowed to see.

What follows from this

  • A null result from your own alumni is close to uninformative. The stricter your selection, the more certain you are to find nothing — so "we checked and it didn't predict anything" is the expected outcome whether the signal works or not.
  • The fix costs almost nothing. Keep the score distribution for everyone who applied, not just those you took — the count, the average and the spread. That is three numbers per signal per year, no personal data, and it is enough to correct for this.
  • Do it now rather than later. Those distributions are usually deleted with the applications a few months after a round closes. Once gone, this is not recoverable for that cohort.
  • It also explains disagreement between programmes. A programme accepting 40% will see a stronger relationship than one accepting 5%, on identical underlying data. Comparing raw figures across programmes without correcting for this compares admissions policies, not signals.
The arithmetic, and how much to trust it

The two big numbers are exact, not read off the pictures. Selecting the top slice of a bell-shaped score shrinks its spread by a known amount, and the visible relationship shrinks with it in a known way — so the widget computes them from the acceptance rate directly. The smaller figures printed on each panel are measured from the dots on screen, which is why they wobble when you draw a different year. That wobble is real and worth noticing: a single cohort is a small sample.

This is the textbook case where selection happens on the score itself. Real decisions involve more than one number, which softens the effect somewhat but does not remove it. The correction and its variants are set out in Sackett & Yang (2000), Correction for range restriction: an expanded typology, Journal of Applied Psychology 85(1):112-118; the requirement it places on you is in methodology.md §3.5 and §7.

The vertical axis stands for whatever outcome measure you use — a rung on the ladder, months to placement, a rating. The argument does not depend on which.

These are simulated applicants, not data from any programme.
