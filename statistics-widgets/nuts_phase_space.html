Steps / ESS is the number of gradient evaluations per effective sample. It is the cost of one draw's worth of independent information, and the number to minimise when tuning Δt. ESS is the smaller of the effective sample sizes of q₁ and q₂, from the chain's autocorrelation (Geyer's initial monotone sequence). Only iterations since Δt or max tree depth last changed count, and at least 100 are needed. With a few hundred it is still noisy, so let Turbo run for a while. Hover over it to see the ESS itself.

Mean depth is the number of doublings that were merged into the trajectory. A trajectory of depth d has 2^d points.

Steps / iter counts gradient evaluations per iteration, including any half that was built and then discarded. This is the cost of NUTS.

Mean accept stat averages min(1, e^(−ΔH)) over every point built. It is the quantity Stan's step-size adaptation targets (0.8 by default). Here Δt is fixed.

Mean |Δθ| is how far round the ring each iteration moves the sample, on average. It measures exploration better than acceptance does.

Divergent counts iterations stopped because the energy error exceeded 1000.

1 · Position space q

The doughnut target, a thin ring of radius 2.6. NUTS grows a leapfrog trajectory by doubling it, forwards or backwards in time at random, until its two ends start heading back towards each other. The hatched region is energetically forbidden at the current energy H₀, so q is confined to the annulus between the orange circles.

2 · Momentum space p

The U-turn test lives here. Let Δq = q⁺ − q⁻ be the chord between the trajectory's two ends. The trajectory is still spreading out as long as both end momenta point along Δq: p⁻·Δq ≥ 0 and p⁺·Δq ≥ 0.

The red half-plane is where p·Δq < 0. Once either end's momentum is in it, extending further would bring the ends closer together, so NUTS stops. The test runs only when a subtree is complete, so an end can sit in the zone for a few steps before it is caught.

p along the trajectory direction of Δq U-turn zone p·Δq < 0 dashed rings: |p| = 1, 2, 3 of the N(0, I) momentum distribution

3 · Energy plane (U, K)

Each 4-D state collapsed to its potential and kinetic energy. Energy levels are diagonals. Resampling p is a vertical jump, since U is unchanged. Leapfrog then slides along the diagonal, in both directions from the start point, because NUTS integrates backwards in time as well as forwards.

Shading is the joint density, proportional to e^(−(U+K)) / √U (capped near U = 0). Margins show the densities of K, which is Exp(1) (left), and U, which is Gamma(½, 1) (bottom, drawn at half height). The bars are the U values of the samples.

4 · Trajectory tree

Top: the trajectory laid out by integration time j, the number of leapfrog steps from the start point (negative means integrated backwards). The row of dots is the points. Each bracket above is a U-turn check on a balanced subtree of 2, 4, 8, … points. Every subtree is checked, not only the whole trajectory, and that is what makes NUTS reversible. A failed check (red) inside the half being added throws that whole half away.

Green bars give the probability that each point becomes the next sample. Within a new half, points are weighted by e^(−ΔH). The new half then takes over the pick with probability min(1, W_new / W_old), where W is the summed weight. This favours later doublings and pushes the sample away from the start.

What to look for

  1. Doubling in random directions. Each iteration resamples p and then repeatedly doubles the trajectory. It flips a coin for forwards or backwards in time and adds as many leapfrog steps as the trajectory already has: 1, 2, 4, 8, … In panel 4, points left of 0 were integrated backwards from the start. Integrating in both directions is what lets the start point sit anywhere inside the final trajectory.
  2. The U-turn test. After each doubling NUTS takes the chord Δq = q⁺ − q⁻ between the two ends (dashed in panel 1) and checks the momentum at each end. If p⁻·Δq or p⁺·Δq turns negative, one end has started coming back, so carrying on would mostly retrace ground already covered. Panel 2 shades the half-plane where that happens. On a circle the chord meets the direction of travel at each end at half the arc's angle, so the test trips once the trajectory has gone roughly half way round the ring. At Δt = 0.1 that takes about 56 steps on average, and the sample moves about 45° round the ring per iteration. The depth histogram is bimodal, though. When the fresh momentum points mostly across the ring rather than along it, the trajectory just bounces between the walls. The test then catches the first bounce, after about 4 steps, which shows up as the peak at depth 2. Nobody had to choose a number of leapfrog steps, and the sampler picks a different one for each momentum draw.
  3. Every subtree is checked. The same test runs on every balanced subtree (the brackets in panel 4), not only the whole trajectory. If a U-turn shows up inside the half being added, that half is discarded (grey) and the iteration ends with the trajectory as it was. On this target that is how about a third of iterations end. This is what makes the scheme reversible: starting from any point of the final trajectory, the same tree could have been built with the same probability.
  4. Picking the sample. There is no accept/reject step. The next state is drawn from the points of the trajectory. Within each new half, points are weighted by e^(−ΔH). The new half as a whole then takes over the pick with probability min(1, W_new / W_old). Each new half is as large as everything before it, so this is usually close to 1 and the pick tends to land in the last doubling, far from the start. Watch the green bars in panel 4 move outward with each merge.
  5. Step size and tree depth. Across the ring the potential is a stiff spring: U = (r − R)²/σ² makes q oscillate radially with angular frequency ω = √(2/σ²) ≈ 7.8, about 8 leapfrog steps per wobble at Δt = 0.1, while it drifts slowly round the ring. Halve Δt and the tree grows one level deeper to cover the same arc: steps per iteration double, but the jumps stay just as long. Raise Δt to 0.2–0.25 and ΔH grows, so the weights become uneven and the accept stat falls to about 0.8 and then 0.45, although the sample still moves well. Leapfrog is only stable for ω·Δt < 2, which here means Δt < √2·σ ≈ 0.26. Past that the radial oscillation blows up, trajectories diverge or U-turn after one or two steps, and the sampler barely moves. Watch Mean |Δθ| collapse. To see the trade-off, run Turbo at a few values of Δt and watch Steps / ESS. It falls from about 720 at Δt = 0.025 and about 225 at 0.1 to roughly 75–90 at 0.2–0.25, then jumps to about 900 at 0.275, just past the stability limit. Larger steps are cheaper, right up to the point where they fail. Stan adapts Δt until the accept stat averages 0.8. Here that happens at about Δt = 0.2, close to the optimum but with some margin before the limit. Lower the max tree depth to 4 to see trajectories cut off before they U-turn (orange in the depth histogram): each iteration then only moves about 12° round the ring.
  6. A neat fact about this target. Each energy level U = u is a pair of circles, at r = R ± σ√u. Their circumferences 2π(R ± σ√u) add up to 4πR whatever u is (as long as the inner circle exists, u < R²/σ² ≈ 205), so the r-dependence cancels: under the target U(q) is Gamma(½, 1), with density e^(−u)/√(πu) and mean ½. It is not Exp(1), because q is 2-D but the potential only constrains its one radial direction. In the energy plane the joint density is therefore e^(−(U+K))/√U. Unlike the banana, it is no longer constant along an energy line: the 1/√U factor piles density up at small U. The bars under panel 3 (U of the samples) should converge to the Gamma(½, 1) curve.
