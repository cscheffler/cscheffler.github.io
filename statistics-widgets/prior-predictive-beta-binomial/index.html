What this shows

A prior predictive distribution: what the model expects the data to look like before any data are seen. The model here is the globe-tossing golem of Chapter 2 — n trials, each a success with probability p — with a Beta prior standing in for what we believe about p.

A likelihood is not a distribution over x until you say which p to use. The prior says we do not know, so the honest answer averages over all of them, weighting each by how plausible the prior finds it:

p(x | n) = ∫ Binomial(x | n, p) Beta(p | α, β) dp

Conjugacy makes that integral come out in closed form. The result is the Beta-Binomial, plotted in step 3. The three panels are the three parts of that expression, in order.

How to use it

  • α and β — the prior's two shape parameters, 0.1 to 50. They change all three panels: the prior directly, the pale family in step 2 (those are the prior's own slices), and the predictive.
  • n — the number of trials, 1 to 200. It has no effect on the prior; it sets the horizontal range of steps 2 and 3.
  • p — not a parameter of the model. It picks which single member of the likelihood family to draw in bold in step 2, and marks that value on the prior. Sweep it and watch step 2 slide while step 3 stays put: the predictive has already integrated p away.
  • Each slider has a text box for typing an exact value; the arrow keys nudge it, and Shift makes the step ten times bigger.
  • Hover over (or tap) any panel to read off values.
  • auto-scale vertical axes — on by default; switching it off freezes all three axes where they currently sit, which is what to do before changing a parameter to compare heights honestly. Anything above a frozen axis is marked with a caret at the top edge.

Things to try

  • Start at the defaults (α = β = 2, n = 20). The prior is a gentle hump centred on 0.5, yet the predictive is nothing like Binomial(x | 20, 0.5): it is more than twice as wide and almost flat. Uncertainty about p is uncertainty about x, and it does not stay small.
  • Walk α and β up together — (2,2), (10,10), (50,50). The prior narrows onto 0.5, the pale family in step 2 collapses onto a single curve, and the predictive closes in on a single Binomial. A sharp prior on p is the only thing that makes the Binomial at the prior mean an honest summary of what to expect.
  • Set α = β = 1, the uniform prior. The predictive is exactly flat: every count from 0 to n is equally likely, each with probability 1/(n+1). Knowing nothing about p means expecting nothing in particular about x.
  • Set α = β = 0.5, the Jeffreys prior, or push both below 1. The prior piles up at both ends and the predictive follows: a U-shape over the counts, expecting nearly all successes or nearly none. This is worth seeing before adopting a "vague" prior — vague on p can be a strong claim about the data.
  • Set α = 1, β = 9 and n = 100. The predictive puts most of its mass on small counts but keeps a long tail. Then move p to 0.5 and note that step 2's bold curve sits in a region the prior has all but ruled out — which is why it contributes almost nothing to the average.

Notes

  • The prior predictive is BetaBinomial(x | n, α, β) = C(n,x) B(x+α, n−x+β) / B(α, β), evaluated in log space with log Γ from a Lanczos approximation. Nothing here is simulated, so the plot does not wobble and there is no seed to set.
  • Its mean is nα/(α+β) — the same as the Binomial at the prior mean. Its standard deviation is larger by a factor √((α+β+n)/(α+β+1)) — compare the two standard deviations reported under steps 2 and 3. That factor is 1 only when n = 1, and grows without bound as n does: this extra spread is what "overdispersion" means.
  • The twelve pale Binomials in step 2 are at the prior's twelve equal-probability slices (its quantiles at 1/24, 3/24, …, 23/24, found by inverting the incomplete beta function). Each one is joined up with a thin line so that twelve overlaid Binomials read as twelve curves rather than as scatter; the dots are the actual values, and appear whenever there is room for them. They are a visual aid only — step 3 is the exact integral, not the average of those twelve.
  • The prior is a density, so its height is not capped at 1 and it diverges at an endpoint when α or β is below 1. Its auto-scaled axis is therefore taken from the central 98% of the range and the rest is clipped and marked with carets, exactly as in the Beta widget. Steps 2 and 3 are probabilities, capped at 1.
