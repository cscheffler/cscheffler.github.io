Why this happens

The loss is smallest when the line falls in the middle of the group and grows fast as it moves towards either end. That matters here specifically: around 80% of fellowship alumni are already working in AI safety, so the usual yes/no line sits a long way off-centre — the worst place for it to be. Push the slider towards a rare outcome and watch the last column climb.

You cannot fix this with a better application process. How much a collapse costs barely depends on how well your signals predict anything — it depends on where the line sits. A programme with genuinely excellent selection loses about as much by recording the outcome as a yes/no as one with no signal at all. There is no escaping it except by not collapsing.

What follows from this

  • Record how far someone got, not whether they cleared a line. You can always compute the yes/no afterwards. You cannot go the other way.
  • The line you happened to pick is doing more work than your analysis is. Moving it a few months, or one rung, changes the answer more than most of the signals you are trying to test.
  • This is free to fix. Unlike almost everything else in this project it needs no extra data collection — only that the grade is written down before it is collapsed.
  • Your existing headline number survives. See W6, which shows how to keep reporting it.
The arithmetic, and how much to trust it

The dots are a cohort of the size you chose. The two figures on the right are not that cohort's own numbers — a single cohort of a couple of hundred people swings by a wide margin from draw to draw, and quoting one draw would be quoting luck. They are computed on a large reference cohort at the same settings, so they show what collapsing costs on average where you have put the line. Drawing a different cohort changes the picture, not the arithmetic.

Each simulated person gets an application signal and an outcome that is partly driven by it and partly by everything else. "Signal that survives" compares how strongly the application signal tracks the full outcome with how strongly it tracks the collapsed yes/no. Both are measured on ranks — on the order people came in rather than the raw numbers — so that the comparison isolates the cost of collapsing and is not distorted by the fact that months to placement is a lopsided scale with a long tail. The thrown-away figure converts that into people: halving the strength of a relationship costs the same as losing three quarters of your sample, because precision scales with the square.

For a bell-shaped outcome the loss is exactly known — collapsing at the midpoint retains about 80% of the relationship, which is equivalent to discarding roughly a third of the sample, and a 90/10 split retains under 60%. See MacCallum, Zhang, Preacher & Rucker (2002), On the practice of dichotomization of quantitative variables, Psychological Methods 7(1):19-40, and Cohen (1983), The cost of dichotomization, Applied Psychological Measurement 7(3):249-253.

The ladder levels are those drafted in methodology.md §4.1. The simulation is seeded, so a given link always shows the same cohort; "draw a different cohort" changes the seed and the seed travels in the URL.

These are simulated people, not data from any programme. The widget shows the shape of the problem, not a measurement of anyone.
