Prompt History for Research Manager Training Curriculum

This is the prompt history for creating the initial Technical AI Safety Research Manager Training curriculum. This interaction happened shortly after Anthropic made the Fable 5 model available for public use under their paid plans. The resulting curriculum is on GitHub.

Initial prompt

The goal of this course is to create a programme to train a really great Research Manager (RM) in Technical AI Safety over a 26-week period. This is to be a very hands on programme. The RM will mentor ~8 researchers-in-training in Technical AI Safety as they are working through this course. The goal for the researchers-in-training is to produce a high quality research paper, strong enough to stand a good chance of getting accepted to NeurIPS, ICML, or similar conference proceedings or journal in the field. The research-in-training will also produce supporting artifacts, including a fully reproducible code repository, an accessible (to a wider but still technical audience) blog/forum post, and a preferably interactive website showcasing their findings.

The RM has to learn the right content at the right time to guide their mentees through the process of identifying, narrowing down, and derisking a topic. The topic might shift substantially as they derisk - this is normal. The RM then has to guide their mentees through building on existing work (perhaps reproducing some results) with the goal of producing new research, then iterating on that new research to produce something substantial (sufficient for a conference submission) by the end of the 26-week period. The RM will also provide guidance around writing the paper and supporting artifacts, writing style, writing efficiently, producing high-quality figures, a repo, and supporting website and blog/forum post.

While the RM is guiding their mentees, they also need to learn (mostly through readings), all the important conceptual knowledge underpinning AI safety and Technical AI safety. Review https://chatgpt.com/share/6a4a7818-b000-83eb-a5d7-6795b400d2c8 for context and general background — treat this as a starting point and not the final word on what needs to be in the curriculum.

With all of the above and the resources you reviewed, use your judgment to decide what must be prioritised to train a great RM in Technical AI Safety within 26 weeks. This really is the primary goal. The RM also needs practical things to do with their mentees each week, including regular check-ins, providing the right guidance at the right time, and giving them resources to read or review for their own learning. To get this right, you might want to develop 2 parallel curricula — one for the RM and one for the researchers-in-training, linking them together.

Let me know if you have any questions before diving into developing the programmes.

Questions Claude Asked

What is the RM’s starting profile? This determines how much of the 26 weeks goes to ML/technical foundations versus safety concepts and research-management craft.

The RM might already have some ML research background and/or software engineering background and/or experience manager either researchers or other people. Generally, the RM will have some subset of these skills and it’s hard to know/predict which. So, the curriculum needs to be clear about what concepts/materials are important for RMs of all backgrounds and which resources are optional and can be skipped if the RM already has particular skills. Provide clear guidance about what each resource or activity is for so the RM can decide whether or not they need it. Assume a high level of meta-cognitive skills and that the RM can assess what they need and don’t need, given the information about what the purpose of a resource is.

Who are the ~8 researchers-in-training, and how much time do they have per week?

Students, part-time (~10-15h)

How should the 8 mentees be organised for the research output?

Individual projects

What compute/resources will the researchers have access to? This constrains which research areas are feasible for a strong paper.

The aim is to have a budget of up to $1000 per student (so $8000 in total) for API credits and/or cloud GPU compute. How the money is spent and how much is needed (up to the $1k cap) will depend on each student’s project.

Claude’s Summary After v1 Build

The programme is built — 15 files in the repo (uncommitted, so you can review first): two linked curricula, an RM operating playbook, a week-by-week spine tying all three threads together, an annotated resource list, and eight working templates.

How the pieces fit. programme/30-week-by-week.md is the spine: for each of the 26 weeks it aligns what the researchers are doing, what mentoring skill the RM is deliberately practising on them, and what the RM is studying — timed so each RM skill lands 1–2 weeks before the mentees need it (vetting craft before proposals in week 6, review-running and hard-conversation prep before the week-13 midpoint, editing craft before drafts land in week 20). The RM curriculum opens with a written self-assessment diagnostic, and every resource carries Purpose / Skip if / Time annotations, per your point about the RM’s unpredictable background. The playbook is the operational half: 1:1 protocol, all 26 group sessions, the 7-question project-vetting rubric with a worked example, milestone protocols, a RAG portfolio-triage system, and a diagnosis table of researcher failure modes.

The design choices worth knowing about:

  • The whole thing is organised against the “Trees are mostly made of air” failure mode from your ChatGPT conversation: foundations must be exercised, not stored. The RM writes weekly 500-word concept notes and monthly red-teams one project’s theory of change; researchers must defend “why does this reduce AI risk?” at proposal, midpoint, and in the paper’s introduction.
  • Individual projects × part-time students is the riskiest configuration you could have picked, so the structure compensates: buddy pairs as first-line support, a hard “last responsible pivot point” at week 13, an RM-maintained bank of pre-derisked topics as the safety net, and strict RM timeboxes so eight solo projects don’t dissolve the week. I’ve also been honest in the docs that main-track acceptance is the stretch outcome — TMLR and top workshops are the credible floor, and the programme optimises for submission-ready quality with the venue decided at week 24.
  • The $1k budgets are milestone-gated ($150 → $500 → $1,000 unlocked at proposal and midpoint), which structurally forces cheap derisking before expensive commitment; the genre menu pre-scopes topics to what’s feasible at that budget (evals, control, black-box behavioural science, small-model interpretability, model organisms).
  • Every programme structure trains the RM twice — the midpoint review exists for the researchers and is the RM’s first full review cycle; the week-26 deliverable is the RM’s own written handbook, which doubles as their portfolio piece for RM roles.

Key sources verified and linked throughout: AI Safety Atlas (by CeSIA), ARENA with materials at learn.arena.education, and the AI Safety, Ethics & Society textbook.

Two things I’d flag for your review: the week-by-week assumes the RM can put in roughly 16–18 mentee-facing hours plus 6–10 study hours weekly (stated in the overview — worth checking against reality), and the group-session calendar assumes all 8 researchers share a timezone-compatible slot. Happy to adjust either, add a facilitator variant for async cohorts, or commit this as an initial commit when you’ve had a look.

Follow-up Prompt

The schedules for the mentees and the RM is a bit brutal at the moment, but the rest looks great. Let’s reduce the time commitment, assuming ~10h (not 10–15h) for the mentees, and ~12h (not 16–18h) mentee-facing hours for the RM plus study hours. These are average per week durations. If this means we reduce the group meetings from 1 per week to one every 2-3 weeks, that is fine. Weekly async check-ins and/or deliverables is still necessary. It could be that more time is offloaded to peer review/feedback/ideating, with a summary sent async to the RM for further feedback. If there are resources or activities that are being scrapped but would be useful, mark them as optional/extension so they can be done by mentees and/or RMs who are more experienced and have time for them. (This optional/extension approach is good generally; feel free to add more resources, activities as long as their purpose is explained and they are marked as optional/extension.)

Another change I’d like to make, that will add some time back in, is that the RM has a 40-hour head start before the mentees. This will probably take the form of 4 weeks at 10 hours per week, but could also be done in 2 weeks or even an intense full-time week. Move any ramp-up material in here, leaving more space in the 26-week mentoring programme to review other, possible optional material, and to spend time with or giving feedback to mentees.

Follow-up Prompt

Now, create a facilitators guide for guiding the RM (who will, in turn, guide the mentees). This guide can be used by a senior RM who will provide light but regular guidance to new RMs, as needed. The guide should provide an outline of the programme, a week-by-week timeline, and specific risks to look out for along with the timeline. Feel free to add other information that would be useful. The guide should also be legible to the new RMs, assuming they are sufficiently self-motivated and have enough perspective on their own learning to use it. Let me know if you have any questions.

Follow-up Prompt

In your judgment, is there anything we need to add, (re)organise, or clarify? Or are we ready to start the programme with our first, highly motivated new RM?

My judgment: you’re close to launch-ready — the documentation is complete for running the programme, but three gaps would genuinely bite if left unfixed, all cheap to add. Everything else I considered adding I’d deliberately leave out.

Worth adding before week 1:

  1. An authorship and credit policy. Nothing in the docs says whether the RM is a co-author, what pod-mates and buddies get, or who owns the code. This bites at weeks 20–26, when the papers become real and retrofitting credit norms breeds resentment. It needs about half a page: mentee is lead author by default; RM co-authorship only for substantive intellectual contribution beyond mentoring (decided openly at proposal time, not at submission); pods/buddies acknowledged by default; repos open-source by default (which the reproducibility bar already implies); AI-assistance disclosure per venue policy.

  2. Cohort intake criteria and an expectations agreement. The programme assumes 8 suitable mentees appear, but attrition mitigation starts at selection. Worth a short section: screen for realistically available hours (ask to see their weekly calendar against their course load, don’t ask “do you have 10 hours”), working Python, a writing sample, and motivation they can articulate. Then a one-page week-1 agreement covering time expectations, the openness norms (everything visible to buddy/pod/RM), budget rules, the pivot/descope rules, and the credit policy — signed before anyone gets attached to a topic.

  3. A concrete Phase 0 ops checklist. “Ops setup, 6h” currently leaves the RM to invent the stack. The checklist is short but load-bearing: where check-ins and pod summaries live; per-student API keys with hard spend caps (right now the budget gates are enforced entirely by self-report — spend caps are the only technical backstop); a mentee repo template; all 26 weeks of calendar invites placed before week 1; where recordings of cohort sessions go.

Worth a one-line clarification each: the session calendar assumes a shared timezone slot (if the cohort is distributed, cluster pods by timezone and record cohort sessions); pod handout prep belongs in the RM’s session-prep budget the week before each format debuts; and an AI-use norm for mentees — using models for coding and editing is fine and expected in 2026, but “you must be able to explain every line and every sentence you submit” is pedagogically load-bearing and belongs in the expectations agreement.

Things I considered and would not add: more readings or activities (both hour budgets are at capacity — additions now have negative value); a pre-built library of pod handouts (making them is genuine RM training; a library is a nice second-cohort artifact); a retrospective survey template; an RM-continuity plan (the dashboard, logs, and handbook already are one).

Two launch-checklist items that sit outside the docs: line up the senior RM or explicitly invoke the guide’s solo mode with a named calibration buddy, and pick the start date against the venue calendar — starting mid-July puts week 26 in mid-January 2027, which lands submission-ready papers right around a typical ICML deadline, with NeurIPS in May as the comfortable fallback and TMLR always open (verify against the live calendar per the overview).

One honest caveat on “ready”: the remaining risk isn’t documentation — it’s live judgment at the two moments the docs can’t make for anyone: the week-6 proposal gate and the week-13 descope calls. That’s precisely what the facilitator guide’s sampling weeks exist to backstop, so with a senior RM (or calibration buddy) actually booked for weeks 6 and 13, I’d call it ready.

Say the word and I’ll add the three items and the clarifications — it’s roughly a page and a half of edits — and then this is ready for its first RM.

Do it.
