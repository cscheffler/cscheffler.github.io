The Beta distribution — Beta(p | α, β)

A continuous probability distribution over a probability. Two shape parameters, α and β, bend it into anything from flat, to a gentle hump, to a spike, to a bathtub piled up at both ends.

What this shows

The probability density of the Beta distribution, Beta(p | α, β). Where the Binomial describes counts, the Beta describes a probability: it puts a distribution on the very quantity p that the Binomial takes as given. That makes it the usual choice of prior for p, and the shape a posterior takes once Binomial data arrive.

Read α and β as weights of evidence pulling in opposite directions: α toward p = 1, β toward p = 0. Their ratio sets where the mass sits, and their total sets how tightly it is concentrated.

This is a density, not a probability, so its height is not capped at 1 and the vertical axis has no upper limit. What is fixed is the area underneath: exactly 1, always. That is the difference between this curve and the Binomial likelihood curve, which has area 1/(n+1) and is not a distribution over p at all.

How to use it

  • α and β — the two shape parameters, from 0.1 to 50. Raise them together and the curve narrows; raise one relative to the other and the mass slides toward that end.
  • Each slider has a text box beside it for typing an exact value; the arrow keys nudge it by 0.1, and Shift makes the step ten times bigger.
  • Hover over (or tap) the plot to read off p and the density there.
  • The dashed line marks the mean, α/(α+β).
  • auto-scale vertical axis — on by default. Switch it off to freeze the axis, which is what to do when comparing how concentrated two different Betas are. Anything above a frozen axis is marked with a caret at the top edge; hovering still reads its true value.

Things to try

  • α = β = 1 gives a flat line: every value of p equally dense. This is the uniform prior, and Beta is the family that contains it.
  • α = β = 0.5, the Jeffreys prior. The density piles up at both ends and dips in the middle — a prior that expects p to be near certainty, without saying which way. Below 1, the curve runs off the top of the plot at the edges; the carets mark where.
  • Set α = 7, β = 4 and compare with the Binomial likelihood widget at k = 6, n = 9. The curves have exactly the same shape, because Beta(α, β) is the same function of p as a Binomial likelihood with k = α−1 successes and n−k = β−1 failures. Only the vertical scale differs, since the Beta is normalised to area 1.
  • Walk α and β up together — (2,2), (5,5), (20,20), (50,50) — with auto-scale off after the first. The mean never moves; the curve just gets taller and thinner. That is what accumulating data does to a posterior.
  • α = 1, β = 5. The density is highest exactly at p = 0 and falls away: a prior that thinks the probability is small but will not quite rule anything out.

Notes

  • The density is pα−1(1−p)β−1 divided by the Beta function B(α, β). It is computed in log space, with log Γ from a Lanczos approximation, so non-integer parameters are handled exactly as easily as whole numbers.
  • When α < 1 the density goes to infinity as p approaches 0, and likewise for β < 1 at p = 1. Infinity has no sensible axis, so the auto-scaled axis is taken from the central 98% of the range (p between 0.01 and 0.99) and the part of the curve above it is clipped and marked with carets. The area under the full curve is still 1: the spike is infinitely tall but infinitely thin.
  • The mode is reported only when there is a single interior one, which needs both α and β above 1. Otherwise the density is highest at an endpoint, or at both.
  • The URL updates as you change the inputs, so you can copy it from the address bar and share a particular α and β with someone else. A frozen axis travels with the link too, as &y=, so everyone opening it sees the same scale.
